Question 1

In November 2026, a technician at an independent pharmacy is clearing old receiving files and finds the transaction information and transaction statements for prescription drug shipments received in March 2021. Under federal law, may these papers be discarded now?

  1. Yes; they follow the 2-year DEA record rule
  2. No; keep them 10 years under the 2023 enhanced rules
  3. No; keep them until at least March 2027, six years on
  4. Yes; 5 years have passed, which meets the federal limit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Two years is the DEA retention period for controlled substance records; DSCSA tracing documents have their own, longer federal requirement.
  • B. The enhanced 2023 requirements changed how data are exchanged, not the six-year retention period.
  • C. Correct. DSCSA transaction information and statements are kept for at least six years after the transaction, so March 2021 records stay until at least March 2027.
  • D. Five years falls a year short of the DSCSA period, which runs six years from each transaction.

Under the DSCSA, a dispenser accepts a prescription drug only with transaction information and a transaction statement from the prior owner, and keeps them for not less than six years after the transaction. They may be paper or electronic, and a wholesaler can store them under a written agreement, but the pharmacy stays responsible. The records let the pharmacy answer an FDA or state request during a recall or a suspect product investigation, which it must do within two business days.

Sources: FDA: Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA); 21 CFR Part 1304 (controlled substance records and inventories)

Question 2

In a hospital IV room, a technician finishes preparing a dose of arsenic trioxide (Trisenox), a P-listed drug, and is left with a single-dose vial that still holds some solution. Under the EPA's hazardous waste pharmaceuticals rule, how should the technician discard the vial?

  1. Place it in a closed container marked Hazardous Waste Pharmaceuticals
  2. Send it to the reverse distributor with the expired stock for credit
  3. Place it in the yellow trace chemotherapy bin with the used gown and gloves
  4. Empty the remainder into the sink drain, then put the vial in a sharps container

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A vial that still holds a P-listed drug is not empty, so it is a non-creditable hazardous waste pharmaceutical and goes into a closed container marked with those words.
  • B. Reverse distributors take potentially creditable items, which must be undispensed and in original manufacturer packaging with a real chance of credit; a partly used vial has no expectation of credit.
  • C. Arsenic trioxide is an antineoplastic, so the trace bin feels right, but trace containers are meant for items that are already empty; a vial with drug left in it has to be managed as hazardous waste.
  • D. Tempting because an empty vial is easier to discard, but the EPA bans every healthcare facility from sewering hazardous waste pharmaceuticals, so the drain is the one place the solution cannot go.

Under 40 CFR Part 266 Subpart P, a vial counts as empty only when its contents have been removed by the usual means, so a partly used vial of a P-listed drug (arsenic trioxide is P012) is a non-creditable hazardous waste pharmaceutical. It goes into a container marked "Hazardous Waste Pharmaceuticals", kept closed and secured against unauthorized access, and later leaves with a hazardous waste transporter. The sewer ban in the same subpart applies to all healthcare facilities, including very small quantity generators, because drugs poured down a drain pass through treatment plants into waterways. The Trisenox label itself says to discard unused portions of each vial properly.

Sources: EPA: Management of Hazardous Waste Pharmaceuticals; FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 3

A long-term care pharmacy mailed a DEA Form 222 for Focalin (dexmethylphenidate) 10 mg tablets, 100-count bottles, to its wholesaler two weeks ago. The wholesaler says it never received the form, and nothing has shipped. Under federal law, how should the pharmacy replace the order?

  1. Send the wholesaler a photocopy of the pharmacy's copy
  2. Send a new form with a statement on the lost form
  3. Ask the wholesaler to fill the order from a phone call
  4. Report the lost form to DEA on Form 106 first

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because the pharmacy kept a copy of the form it sent, but the supplier fills only the original Form 222, and a copy cannot stand in for a lost original.
  • B. Correct. The purchaser executes a new Form 222 and attaches a statement giving the order form number and date of the lost form and stating that the goods were not received because that form was lost; a copy of the statement goes to the supplier with the new form.
  • C. Tempting because it would restore supply quickly, but a Schedule II order between registrants needs a Form 222 or a CSOS order; a phone request cannot replace one.
  • D. Tempting because Form 106 is DEA's loss report, but it covers controlled substances that are stolen or lost, not an order form lost in the mail; a Form 222 lost in transit is replaced with a new form and a statement.

Under 21 CFR 1305.16(a), when an unfilled Form 222 is lost, the purchaser executes another one and attaches a statement with the order form number and date of the lost form, saying the goods it covered were not received because of the loss. The pharmacy keeps a copy of the new form and the statement with its copy of the first form, and if the lost form later reaches the supplier, the supplier marks it 'Not accepted' and returns it. Blank forms stolen or lost at the pharmacy are handled differently: they are reported at once to the DEA Special Agent in Charge with their serial numbers.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1305 (DEA Form 222 and CSOS orders); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022)

Question 4

A prescription for morphine sulfate 15 mg immediate-release tablets, #60, was written on November 2. On November 3 the patient asks a community pharmacy to fill only 20 tablets now because she is worried about keeping extra at home, and state law allows patient-requested partial fills. Under federal law, what is the last date the remaining 40 tablets may be dispensed?

  1. November 6, 72 hours after the first partial fill
  2. December 2, 30 days after the date written
  3. December 3, 30 days after the first partial fill
  4. January 1, 60 days after the date written

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because 72 hours is a real Schedule II limit, but it applies when the pharmacy cannot supply the full quantity, not when the patient asks for less.
  • B. Correct. Under 21 CFR 1306.13(b), the DEA rule that carries out the 2016 CARA partial fill provision, the remaining portions of a Schedule II prescription partially filled at the patient's or prescriber's request must be filled no later than 30 days after the date written: November 2 + 30 days = December 2.
  • C. Tempting because the partial filling started on November 3, but the 30 days are counted from the date the prescription was written.
  • D. Tempting because 60 days from issue is the limit for partial fills for terminally ill or long-term care facility patients, which does not describe this patient.

Federal law has three Schedule II partial fill rules. If the pharmacy is short, the balance must be supplied within 72 hours of the first partial fill, or the pharmacist notifies the prescriber and no more may be dispensed without a new prescription (21 CFR 1306.13(a)). If the patient, a parent or caregiver acting for the patient, or the prescriber requests a partial fill, the rest may be filled up to 30 days after the date written (1306.13(b)). For a long-term care facility patient or a patient with a documented terminal illness, partial fills may continue for up to 60 days from the issue date (1306.13(c)). The total dispensed cannot exceed the quantity prescribed, and a stricter state rule controls.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1306 (controlled substance prescriptions); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 5

FDA's current DSCSA exemption for small business dispensers runs through November 27, 2027 and counts the owner's full-time pharmacists and pharmacy technicians across all its stores. Which pharmacy falls outside the exemption and must already meet the enhanced electronic, package-level tracing requirements?

  1. One store; 18 full-time pharmacists and technicians, plus 30 cashiers
  2. Three stores; 35 full-time pharmacists and technicians in total
  3. One store; 20 full-time pharmacists and technicians, plus 15 part-time technicians
  4. One store; exactly 25 full-time pharmacists and technicians

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Cashiers are not counted; 18 full-time pharmacists and technicians is under the threshold of 25, so the store is a small business dispenser.
  • B. Correct. The count is the owning company's total, and 35 exceeds 25, so this owner's stores have had to meet the enhanced requirements since the larger-dispenser exemption ended on November 27, 2025.
  • C. The exemption counts full-time employees (an average of at least 30 hours a week), so the part-time technicians do not push this store past 25.
  • D. The exemption covers an owner with 25 or fewer full-time pharmacists and technicians, so exactly 25 still qualifies; the larger-dispenser group started at 26.

The enhanced drug distribution security requirements, interoperable electronic exchange of transaction data and package-level tracing and verification, took effect November 27, 2023, followed by a stabilization period and FDA exemptions staggered by trading partner type. Dispensers with 26 or more full-time employees had until November 27, 2025. FDA defines a small business dispenser as one whose owning company has 25 or fewer full-time employees licensed as pharmacists or qualified as pharmacy technicians, and in August 2026 extended that exemption to November 27, 2027. Exempt pharmacies must still quarantine and investigate suspect product.

Sources: FDA: Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA)

Question 6

During an outdate pull at a community pharmacy, a technician finds expired bottles of clindamycin phosphate topical solution 1%. The label lists isopropyl alcohol 50% v/v as the vehicle, and clindamycin is not on the EPA's P or U list. Under federal EPA rules, why is this product still a hazardous waste?

  1. It is ignitable because of its alcohol content
  2. It is toxic because it leaches heavy metals
  3. It is corrosive because of its acidic pH
  4. It is reactive because it is a phosphate ester

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A liquid with a flash point below 60 °C is ignitable (D001) unless it holds less than 24% alcohol, and this solution is 50% isopropyl alcohol.
  • B. The toxicity characteristic covers leachable contaminants such as mercury, arsenic or selenium, and none is an ingredient of this product.
  • C. Corrosive waste is aqueous with a pH of 2 or less or 12.5 or more; this solution is adjusted to pH 4 to 7, so corrosivity does not apply.
  • D. Reactive wastes are unstable, can explode or give off toxic gas with water; the phosphate ester of clindamycin does none of these, so the chemical name is a red herring.

A drug that is not P- or U-listed can still be a hazardous waste if it shows one of four characteristics: ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity or toxicity (40 CFR 261.21-261.24). Under 261.21(a)(1), a liquid with a flash point below 60 °C (140 °F) is ignitable, except a solution with less than 24% alcohol by volume and at least 50% water by weight. A 50% isopropyl alcohol vehicle is far above that cut-off, so the expired bottles go into the hazardous waste pharmaceuticals container, and Subpart P requires a container of ignitable waste to be managed so that it cannot cause a fire or release flammable fumes.

Sources: EPA: Management of Hazardous Waste Pharmaceuticals; FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 7

A medical assistant from a nearby oral surgery practice, which holds its own DEA registration, brings expired midazolam 1 mg/mL vials (Schedule IV) from the practice's procedure-room stock to a community pharmacy that is a DEA-authorized collector. She asks to drop them in the pharmacy's take-back receptacle. What should the technician do?

  1. Accept them, because the receptacle takes Schedule II to V drugs
  2. Decline, because a registrant's own stock may not be collected
  3. Accept them after she shows photo ID and signs the receptacle log
  4. Accept them once the practice completes a DEA Form 41 for the vials

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because the schedule fits, but the receptacle collects only drugs lawfully possessed by an ultimate user or another authorized non-registrant, such as a person disposing of a decedent's property.
  • B. Correct. A collection receptacle takes controlled substances lawfully possessed by ultimate users and other authorized non-registrants; a registered practice disposes of its inventory through the registrant disposal routes instead.
  • C. Tempting because ID checks and logbooks are routine for pseudoephedrine sales, but no ID or signature makes registrant stock eligible, and collectors keep no record of what is deposited.
  • D. Tempting because Form 41 records destroyed controlled substances, but filling it out does not turn a registrant's inventory into something a collector may take.

Collection receptacles exist for ultimate users. Under 21 CFR 1317.75(b) and (c), a collector may accept only Schedule II to V drugs lawfully possessed by an ultimate user or another authorized non-registrant, such as a person lawfully entitled to dispose of a decedent's property. A DEA registrant like this practice disposes of its own inventory under 21 CFR 1317.05: on-site destruction to a non-retrievable state recorded on DEA Form 41, delivery to a reverse distributor, return to its supplier or manufacturer, or a request for help from the DEA field office. The same rule keeps the pharmacy's own expired stock out of its receptacle, and the technician brings in the pharmacist to explain.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1317 (controlled substance disposal); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 8

A technician at a pharmacy that fills many Medicaid prescriptions is entering a new patient's intake form. Under the OBRA 90 drug use review rules, which item is part of the patient profile the pharmacist must make a reasonable effort to obtain and record?

  1. An emergency contact's phone number
  2. Household income and employer
  3. Known allergies and drug reactions
  4. Social Security number

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because the patient's own telephone number is on the list, but a separate emergency contact is not one of the required profile elements.
  • B. Tempting because income can matter for assistance programs, but financial and employment details are not part of the drug use review profile.
  • C. Correct. The federal profile list includes significant individual history such as disease states, known allergies and drug reactions, and a list of medications and devices.
  • D. Tempting because many intake forms ask for it, but it is not on the OBRA 90 profile list, and collecting more identifiers than needed adds privacy risk.

OBRA 90 made states require prospective drug use review for Medicaid prescriptions, an offer to counsel and patient profiles. Under 42 CFR 456.705(d) the pharmacist must make a reasonable effort to obtain, record and maintain the patient's name, address, telephone number, date of birth or age and gender; significant history including diseases, allergies, drug reactions and a list of medications and devices; and the pharmacist's comments. Technicians usually collect these data, which is why accurate allergy entry matters: the screening alerts it triggers go to the pharmacist to resolve.

Sources: 42 CFR 456 Subpart K (OBRA 90 drug use review)

Question 9

A hospital pharmacy sorts its expired injectables by EPA waste listing. Each product below has a single active ingredient and is a listed hazardous waste. Under federal EPA rules, which one is on the P-list of acute hazardous wastes?

  1. Mitomycin
  2. Melphalan
  3. Cyclophosphamide
  4. Physostigmine salicylate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Mitomycin C is U010; most listed chemotherapy drugs sit on the U-list, which is why a cancer drug is the near-miss here.
  • B. Melphalan is a cytotoxic alkylating agent that feels as if it should be acute, but it is U150, a U-listed waste.
  • C. Cyclophosphamide is a listed hazardous waste, which makes it tempting, but it is U058 on the U-list of toxic wastes, not the acute P-list.
  • D. Correct. Physostigmine salicylate is P188 (and physostigmine itself is P204), so this anticholinergic-toxicity antidote is an acute hazardous waste when it is discarded.

40 CFR 261.33(e) lists the acute hazardous wastes (P-codes) and 261.33(f) the toxic wastes (U-codes), and a listing applies to any formulation in which the chemical is the sole active ingredient. Physostigmine (P204) and physostigmine salicylate (P188) sit on the P-list with arsenic trioxide (P012), nicotine (P075) and warfarin above 0.3% (P001), while cyclophosphamide (U058), melphalan (U150) and mitomycin C (U010) are U-listed. The split still matters under Subpart P: an IV bag that held a U-listed drug can count as empty under the general RCRA rule, but one that held a P-listed drug is empty only when the dose has been fully administered.

Sources: EPA: Management of Hazardous Waste Pharmaceuticals; FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 10

A technician preparing a short law review for new pharmacy staff is placing federal drug laws on a timeline. Which law FIRST made it a federal offense to ship adulterated or misbranded drugs across state lines, while still requiring no review of a drug before it was sold?

  1. Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951
  2. Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938
  3. Sherley Amendment of 1912
  4. Pure Food and Drugs Act of 1906

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because it is a well-known early amendment, but it defined which drugs need medical supervision and limited them to prescription sale; it came 45 years after the first ban on misbranded drugs.
  • B. Tempting because it is the statute pharmacies still work under, but it replaced the 1906 act and was the first to require that a new drug be shown safe before marketing, so it does not fit 'no review before sale'.
  • C. Tempting because it is the next step on the timeline, but it amended the existing 1906 act to reach false therapeutic claims made to defraud; it did not create the original interstate ban.
  • D. Correct. The 1906 act banned interstate commerce in adulterated and misbranded foods, drinks and drugs, but manufacturers submitted nothing to the government before marketing.

The Pure Food and Drugs Act, signed by Theodore Roosevelt on June 30, 1906, was the first broad federal food and drug law. It outlawed interstate sale of adulterated or misbranded foods, drinks and medicines, but it had no premarket review: firms filed nothing before selling, and the government carried the burden of proving a label false. The 1912 Sherley Amendment reached false therapeutic claims only with proof of intent to defraud, a hurdle the 1938 act removed when it also required proof of safety before marketing.

Sources: FDA: Milestones in U.S. Food and Drug Law
