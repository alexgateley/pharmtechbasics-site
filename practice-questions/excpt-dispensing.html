Question 1

A community pharmacy handles four orders on the same afternoon. Which task is nonsterile compounding under USP <795>, so it needs a compounding record and a beyond-use date assigned under the chapter?

  1. Adding a flavor the labeling does not list to a commercial oral solution
  2. Splitting scored metoprolol tartrate 25 mg tablets for half-tablet doses
  3. Repackaging pyrazinamide tablets into unit-dose blister cards
  4. Reconstituting cefdinir powder with the volume of water on its label

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Adding a component the manufacturer's labeling does not call for alters the product, which USP defines as compounding, because the effect of the flavor on the drug is unpredictable.
  • B. Tablet splitting changes the dose unit but is listed by USP as a practice not subject to <795>.
  • C. Repackaging is also outside <795>; its expiration dating follows repackaging standards, not the compounding BUD table.
  • D. Tempting because it involves mixing, but reconstitution done as the manufacturer's labeling directs is outside the scope of <795>; the label sets the 10-day discard date.

USP <795> defines nonsterile compounding as combining, admixing, diluting, pooling, reconstituting other than as the labeling provides, or otherwise altering a drug product or bulk drug substance. A flavoring the labeling does not list can interact with the drug, the vehicle or the preservative in ways no one has tested, which is why USP treats adding one as compounding. The chapter lists reconstitution per the labeling, repackaging, tablet splitting and administration as practices it does not cover. Cefdinir's label, for example, gives the water volume for each bottle and a 10-day room-temperature discard date after mixing.

Sources: USP General Chapters <795>, <797> and <800>; Cefdinir for oral suspension prescribing information (2021); Metoprolol tartrate tablets label (Advagen, 2026); NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis)

Question 2

A community pharmacy technician who places the store's diabetic supply orders gets an offer from an online reseller: unopened boxes of a major brand's blood glucose test strips at half the wholesaler's price, described as surplus bought back from patients. Which response is BEST?

  1. Buy them for cash-paying patients so that no insurer is billed
  2. Decline and tell the pharmacist about the offer
  3. Buy the boxes that have intact seals and current expiration dates
  4. Buy them and run control solution on one vial from each lot

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because it seems to avoid a billing problem, but the risk the FDA describes is inaccurate readings, which harms a cash-paying patient as much as an insured one.
  • B. Correct. The FDA advises using only new test strips authorized for sale in the United States and warns against pre-owned strips, so the stock is declined and the pharmacist, who oversees purchasing, is told.
  • C. Tempting because a sealed, in-date box looks new, but a seal does not show how the strips were stored, whether they were tampered with, or whether they were cleared for US sale.
  • D. Tempting because the FDA does advise control-solution checks of a meter and its strips, but one vial cannot show the storage history of every box or whether the strips are authorized for US sale.

The FDA tells users to always use new test strips that are authorized for sale in the United States and has warned about pre-owned strips, which are unused strips previously owned by someone else and resold at lower prices. Such strips may have been stored improperly, may be expired or tampered with, may carry traces of blood if a vial was opened, and may not be cleared by the FDA for US sale, and any of these can give incorrect glucose results that lead to wrong insulin doses. Buying through the pharmacy's usual authorized suppliers avoids the problem.

Sources: FDA: Blood Glucose Monitoring Devices (2024)

Question 3

A pharmacist asks a technician to set up a dose estimate for a 10-month-old infant using the age-based rule designed for infants younger than 1 year. Which formula should the technician use?

  1. Weight in pounds ÷ 150 × adult dose
  2. Body surface area in m² ÷ 1.73 × adult dose
  3. Age in months ÷ 150 × adult dose
  4. Age in years ÷ (age in years + 12) × adult dose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because it also divides by 150, but this is Clark's rule, which scales the dose by weight in pounds rather than by age.
  • B. Tempting because BSA is used for children, but this rule needs height and weight to find the body surface area and is not an age-based rule.
  • C. Correct. This is Fried's rule, the age-based rule for infants under 1 year; it assumes an adult dose suits a child of about 150 months, or 12.5 years.
  • D. Tempting because it is also age-based, but this is Young's rule, which is used for children from 1 to 12 years, not for infants.

Fried's rule estimates an infant's dose from age in months: child's dose = age in months ÷ 150 × adult dose. For a 10-month-old and a 300 mg adult dose, 10 ÷ 150 × 300 = 20 mg. The 150 in Fried's rule stands for months, while the 150 in Clark's rule stands for pounds, which is why the two are easy to confuse. Young's rule covers ages 1 to 12, and the BSA rule divides by 1.73 m², the average adult surface area. All of these are estimates; the pharmacist checks any infant dose against the product's labeled dosing.

Sources: NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis)

Question 4

A technician at a community pharmacy is entering a written prescription from a dentist for phentermine 37.5 mg tablets, #30, one each morning, 'for weight loss.' The dentist's DEA registration is current and covers Schedule IV. What should the technician do?

  1. Call the dentist's office and say the pharmacy will not fill it
  2. Hold it for the pharmacist to review before entry
  3. Fill it, because the dentist's DEA registration covers Schedule IV
  4. Dispense a 7-day supply until the dentist confirms the purpose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Refusing is a decision about validity, which the pharmacist makes; the pharmacist may also find a reason the technician does not know about.
  • B. Correct. Weight loss is outside dental treatment, and deciding whether the prescription is valid belongs to the pharmacist, who may contact the dentist.
  • C. A registration lets a dentist prescribe controlled substances, but a valid prescription also has to be issued in the usual course of the dentist's professional practice.
  • D. A short fill still dispenses a controlled substance on a prescription whose validity is in question, and the technician cannot decide to fill it in part.

Under federal law, a controlled substance prescription is valid only when a practitioner authorized by the state and registered with DEA issues it for a legitimate medical purpose in the usual course of professional practice. A dentist's practice covers dental treatment, so phentermine, a Schedule IV anorectic labeled as a short-term adjunct for weight reduction, falls outside it. The pharmacist has a corresponding responsibility for validity, so the technician flags the prescription rather than entering, refusing or partly filling it.

Sources: NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); 21 CFR Part 1306 (controlled substance prescriptions); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 5

A long-term care pharmacy technician prepares a cycle fill of ziprasidone 40 mg capsules, one capsule twice daily. Which pair of administration instructions matches the Geodon prescribing information?

  1. Take at bedtime; may crush and give by feeding tube
  2. Take with food; swallow whole
  3. Take on an empty stomach; swallow whole
  4. Take with food; may open and sprinkle on applesauce

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting in long-term care, where crushing is common, but the label says not to crush or open the capsules and directs taking them with food.
  • B. Correct. The Geodon label directs that capsules be taken by mouth with food and swallowed whole, without opening, crushing or chewing them.
  • C. Tempting because the capsule handling is right, but the label directs taking ziprasidone with food rather than on an empty stomach.
  • D. Tempting because some capsules, such as Effexor XR, may be opened onto applesauce, but the Geodon label says not to open, crush or chew the capsules.

Geodon (ziprasidone) capsules come in 20, 40, 60 and 80 mg and are labeled for schizophrenia and bipolar I disorder. The prescribing information says to give the capsules by mouth with food and to swallow them whole without opening, crushing or chewing. In long-term care, where many residents receive crushed medications, the technician's auxiliary labels and medication administration record notes help the facility avoid crushing a capsule that should not be crushed. A request to give it by feeding tube goes to the pharmacist, because choosing another route or product is a clinical decision.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 6

A patient being treated for opioid use disorder brings a prescription for Suboxone 8 mg/2 mg sublingual film, one film daily, to a community pharmacy. Which auxiliary instruction matches the label?

  1. Cut the film along the line to adjust the dose
  2. Do not cut, chew or swallow the film
  3. Move the film around the mouth until it dissolves
  4. Swallow the film whole with a full glass of water

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because some tablets are scored for splitting, but the label says the film is given whole; different strengths exist for dose changes.
  • B. Correct. The label states the film must be given whole and is not cut, chewed or swallowed; it dissolves under the tongue or inside the cheek.
  • C. Tempting because lozenges are moved around the mouth, but the label says the film should not be moved once it is placed.
  • D. Tempting because most oral doses are swallowed with water, but swallowing the film bypasses the sublingual absorption the product relies on.

Suboxone sublingual film (buprenorphine and naloxone, 2/0.5, 4/1, 8/2 and 12/3 mg) is placed under the tongue or inside the cheek and left in place until it dissolves completely. The label says not to cut, chew or swallow it, not to move it after placement, and not to eat or drink until it has dissolved. Afterward the patient sips water, swishes it around the teeth and gums and swallows, and waits at least an hour before brushing. The technician labels the prescription and the pharmacist counsels.

Sources: SUBOXONE (buprenorphine and naloxone) Sublingual Film Prescribing Information (Indivior, rev 12/2025)

Question 7

A code cart order in the hospital pharmacy reads: epinephrine 1 mg as 1:10,000. The shelf holds epinephrine 0.1 mg/mL 10 mL prefilled syringes and Adrenalin 1 mg/mL 30 mL multiple-dose vials. Which product and volume fill the order as written?

  1. 1 mL from the 1 mg/mL multiple-dose vial
  2. 10 mL of the 0.1 mg/mL syringe
  3. 1 mL of the 0.1 mg/mL syringe
  4. 10 mL from the 1 mg/mL multiple-dose vial

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because 1 mL of 1 mg/mL is 1 mg, but 1 mg/mL is the 1:1,000 strength, ten times more concentrated than the order specifies.
  • B. Correct. 1:10,000 is 1 g in 10,000 mL, or 0.1 mg/mL, so 1 mg is 10 mL, the whole 0.1 mg/mL syringe.
  • C. Tempting because it is the right product, but 1 mL × 0.1 mg/mL is 0.1 mg, a tenth of the ordered dose.
  • D. Tempting because 10 mL matches the correct volume, but 10 mL of 1 mg/mL is 10 mg, a tenfold overdose.

Convert the ratio strength first. 1:10,000 means 1 g in 10,000 mL; 1 g = 1,000 mg, so 1,000 mg ÷ 10,000 mL = 0.1 mg/mL. The ordered 1 mg is then 1 mg ÷ 0.1 mg/mL = 10 mL, the whole prefilled syringe. The two products on the shelf differ tenfold in concentration, because 1 mg/mL is the old 1:1,000 strength. Current single-entity epinephrine labels state mg/mL rather than a ratio, as both of these do, so a technician who meets a ratio on an order converts it before choosing a product and brings any mismatch between order and stock to the pharmacist.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis)

Question 8

In a hospital IV room, a technician reconstitutes a leucovorin calcium for injection 500 mg vial with 50 mL of Sterile Water for Injection, giving 10 mg/mL, because the ordered dose is above 10 mg/m2. Only 20 mL is drawn up for the dose. How should the solution left in the vial be handled?

  1. Refrigerate it and label it for use within 24 hours
  2. Discard it, since sterile-water solution must be used at once
  3. Label it for use within 28 days of the first puncture
  4. Label it for use within 7 days at room temperature

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting as a common default for reconstituted drugs, but the leucovorin label gives no 24-hour refrigerated dating for solution made with sterile water; it says use immediately.
  • B. Correct. The label says leucovorin reconstituted with Sterile Water for Injection is used immediately and any unused portion is discarded.
  • C. Twenty-eight days is the usual dating for a preserved multiple-dose vial, but this product contains no preservative, so it cannot be treated as one.
  • D. Tempting because the label does allow 7 days, but only for solution made with Bacteriostatic Water for Injection, which contains benzyl alcohol; this vial was made with sterile water.

Leucovorin calcium for injection is a preservative-free lyophilized powder, and the diluent decides how long the solution lasts. Made with Bacteriostatic Water for Injection, it may be used within 7 days; made with Sterile Water for Injection, it must be used immediately and the rest discarded. Doses above 10 mg/m2 call for sterile water because of the benzyl alcohol in bacteriostatic water, so a large dose usually means a no-reuse vial. The label also limits IV injection to 160 mg per minute because of the calcium content, and the unmixed vials are stored at controlled room temperature, protected from light.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); USP General Chapters <795>, <797> and <800>

Question 9

A mail-order pharmacy orders allopurinol 100 mg tablets by NDC 16729-134-16, a 500-count bottle from Accord. That size is on backorder, and the buyer asks for the same tablet in a different bottle size from the same labeler. Which NDC should the technician order?

  1. 16729-135-16
  2. 16729-135-17
  3. 16729-134-01
  4. 16729-135-01

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because it keeps the -16 ending of the original order, but the product code 135 is a different product, the 300 mg tablet. A package code means something only together with its product code.
  • B. Tempting because a larger bottle might seem an easy substitute for a backordered one, but this is the 1,000-count bottle of the 300 mg tablet, a different product.
  • C. Correct. The labeler code (16729) and product code (134) match, so this is the same 100 mg tablet; only the package code changes, and -01 is the 100-count bottle.
  • D. Tempting because it is a 100-count bottle from the same labeler, but product code 135 is the 300 mg strength, three times the ordered tablet.

The NDC has three segments: labeler code, product code and package code, here in Accord's 5-3-2 pattern. Under 21 CFR 207.35, a change in strength or dosage form needs a new product code, while a change only in package size or type keeps the product code and gets a new package code. Accord's allopurinol uses 134 for the 100 mg tablet and 135 for the 300 mg tablet, with package codes 10, 01, 16 and 17 for 30, 100, 500 and 1,000 tablets, so 16729-134-01 is the same tablet in a 100-count bottle. Reading the product code first prevents a wrong-strength order that a matching package code can hide. FDA's final rule moves NDCs to a uniform 12-digit format from March 7, 2033.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 207 (drug listing and the NDC); FDA National Drug Code Directory

Question 10

A mail-order pharmacy technician enters a 90-day prescription for latanoprost ophthalmic solution 0.005%: instill 1 drop in each eye every evening, dispense three 2.5 mL bottles. Using the pharmacy's assumption of 20 drops per mL, what days supply should be entered?

  1. 150 days
  2. 90 days
  3. 25 days
  4. 75 days

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because 150 is the total drop count, but dividing it by 1 drop a day treats the order as one eye, and the sig says each eye.
  • B. Tempting because the prescription is written as a 90-day supply, but days supply comes from the volume and the directions; three bottles at this sig fall short of 90 days, a gap to point out to the pharmacist.
  • C. This is the supply of one bottle (2.5 mL × 20 = 50 drops ÷ 2 = 25 days); the order dispenses three.
  • D. Correct. 3 bottles × 2.5 mL = 7.5 mL; 7.5 mL × 20 drops/mL = 150 drops; 1 drop × 2 eyes = 2 drops a day; 150 ÷ 2 = 75 days.

Eye drop days supply is worked from total volume, an assumed drop size and the number of eyes treated. Here 7.5 mL at 20 drops per mL gives 150 drops, and one drop in each eye uses 2 drops a day, so the fill lasts 75 days. The latanoprost label doses one drop in the affected eye or eyes once daily in the evening and says the dose should not exceed once daily, because dosing prostaglandin analogs more often may weaken the pressure-lowering effect. For mail order, storage matters as well: unopened bottles are refrigerated at 2 to 8 °C and protected from light, a bottle may be kept up to 40 °C for no more than 8 days in shipment, and an opened bottle may stay at room temperature up to 25 °C for 6 weeks.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)
