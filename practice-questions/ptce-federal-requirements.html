Question 1

A closed-door long-term care pharmacy is billed for a tote of tramadol 50 mg and alprazolam 0.5 mg bottles. The delivery record shows the tote never came off the truck, and nobody at the pharmacy signed for it. The tote cannot be found. Under federal rules, who reports this loss to the DEA?

  1. The delivery carrier, because the tote went missing on its truck
  2. The wholesaler, because the pharmacy never took custody
  3. Both firms together on one joint Form 106
  4. The pharmacy, because the drugs were invoiced to its DEA number

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A common carrier is not the DEA registrant accountable for the drugs; the shipping registrant reports losses in its shipments.
  • B. Correct. When an in-transit shipment fails to reach its destination, the supplier reports the loss; the purchaser becomes responsible only after it signs for or takes custody of the shipment.
  • C. There is no joint report; one registrant reports, decided by who had custody when the drugs went missing.
  • D. Billing does not transfer custody; the pharmacy never received the tote, so the loss stays with the supplier.

The deciding fact is custody. A supplier reports an in-transit loss of a shipment that never reached the purchaser. Once the pharmacy signs for or takes custody of a delivery, any loss after that point is the pharmacy's to report, with written notice within one business day and a Form 106. So a technician checking in totes should count controlled items before signing and note shortages on the delivery record. Central fill arrangements have their own rule: the pharmacy that hires the carrier reports.

Sources: DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); 21 CFR Part 1301 (DEA registration and security); DEA: Theft or Loss of Controlled Substances (Form 106); Tramadol hydrochloride tablets label (Amneal, 2021)

Question 2

A retail pharmacy that supplies a skilled nursing facility plans to install an automated dispensing system at the facility, stocked with oxycodone, lorazepam and other controlled substances for the residents. Under federal law, which registration must be in place for the system?

  1. A distributor registration, since stock leaves the pharmacy
  2. A separate pharmacy registration at the facility's address
  3. A DEA registration held by the nursing facility itself
  4. The pharmacy's existing registration at its own store

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because restocking moves drugs from the store to another site, but what the pharmacy sends to its own registered systems at long-term care facilities does not count toward the 5% limit that would call for a distributor registration.
  • B. Correct. A retail pharmacy that installs and operates an automated dispensing system at a long-term care facility must hold a separate DEA registration at the location of each facility where its systems are placed.
  • C. Tempting because the drugs sit inside the facility, but federal rules allow only a registered retail pharmacy to install and operate an automated dispensing system at a long-term care facility.
  • D. Tempting because the pharmacy owns and restocks the system, but federal rules treat each facility that holds one of its systems as a separately registered location.

Under 21 CFR 1301.27, a retail pharmacy may install and operate automated dispensing systems at long-term care facilities, and no one other than a registered retail pharmacy may do so. The pharmacy needs a separate registration at each facility where a system is located; if two pharmacies run systems at the same facility, each holds its own registration there. These added registrations carry no application fee, and stock the pharmacy sends to these systems is left out of the 5% distribution limit in 21 CFR 1307.11.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1301 (DEA registration and security); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022)

Question 3

A hospital pharmacy ordered two cartons of hydromorphone 1 mg/mL vials (25 vials each) through CSOS. When the shipment arrives, a technician checks the count against the order with the pharmacist. Under federal law, what receiving record must the pharmacy create?

  1. Lot numbers and expiration dates written on the supplier's original order
  2. An entry in a bound Schedule II receiving log signed by the pharmacist
  3. A copy of the order marked received, sent to DEA within two business days
  4. The quantity and date received, electronically linked to the order

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because lot and expiration data matter for recalls, but federal CSOS rules ask the purchaser for quantity and date received, and there is no paper original to write on.
  • B. Tempting because many pharmacies keep such logs by policy or state rule, but the federal record for a CSOS order is the electronic receipt linked to that order.
  • C. Tempting because a two-business-day report to DEA does exist, but it is the supplier's duty for each electronic order it fills, not the purchaser's receiving record.
  • D. Correct. For an electronic order, the purchaser records the quantity of each item received and the date received, and that record must be electronically linked to the original order and archived.

CSOS is DEA's electronic equivalent of Form 222. Orders are signed with a CSOS digital certificate issued to an eligible person. Under 21 CFR 1305.22(g), when the purchaser receives the shipment it creates a record of the quantity of each item and the date received, electronically linked to the original order and archived; 1305.27 then requires the purchaser to keep the signed order and all linked records for two years. The supplier, not the pharmacy, reports each filled electronic order to DEA within two business days (1305.29).

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1305 (DEA Form 222 and CSOS orders); DEA: Controlled Substance Ordering System (CSOS)

Question 4

A retail pharmacy that is a DEA-authorized collector takes its biennial inventory. Two sealed inner liners from its take-back receptacle are in locked storage awaiting pickup, and three unused liners are in the stockroom. Under federal law, what does the inventory record for the liners?

  1. The weight of each sealed liner and the two employees who sealed it
  2. An estimated count of the controlled drugs in each sealed liner
  3. No liner entries, since collected drugs are not the pharmacy's stock
  4. The number, size and unique ID number of the liners

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The two employees' names and signatures go on the liner removal and storage records; the inventory asks for no weights or witnesses.
  • B. Estimated counts apply to opened Schedule III-V stock bottles; collected drugs are not counted or inventoried, and a sealed liner may not be opened.
  • C. The collected drugs are not counted, but the liners themselves are accountable items, so both unused and sealed liners appear on the collector's inventory.
  • D. Correct. A collector's inventory lists the date and, for unused liners and sealed liners awaiting destruction, the number and size of liners and each liner's unique identification number (21 CFR 1304.11(e)(7)(ii)).

A pharmacy that collects take-back drugs inventories the liners, not what is inside them. Once drugs go into a receptacle they are not counted, sorted, inventoried or individually handled, and a sealed liner is not opened, x-rayed or analyzed. So the biennial inventory records the date, the number and size of liners (for example, two 10-gallon liners) and the unique identification number of each, for unused liners and for sealed liners waiting for destruction. Separate liner records track each liner's acquisition, installation, removal, storage and transfer, signed by two employees at each step after acquisition.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1304 (controlled substance records and inventories); 21 CFR Part 1317 (controlled substance disposal); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022)

Question 5

In a hospital pharmacy, a technician files Schedule IV invoices in the general vendor-invoice binder, flagged so they can be pulled quickly, and suggests filing the Schedule II invoices and Form 222 copies in the same binder. Under federal law, which statement is accurate?

  1. Schedule II records must be kept apart from all other records
  2. Neither may go there; Schedule IV invoices need their own file too
  3. Both may go there, because flagged records are readily retrievable
  4. Both may go there if the binder is kept for at least two years

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Inventories and records of Schedule I and II drugs are kept separately from all other pharmacy records (21 CFR 1304.04(h)(1)); Schedule III-V records may be mixed in if readily retrievable.
  • B. Schedule III-V records may be kept separately or within ordinary business records if readily retrievable, so the flagged Schedule IV invoices meet the rule.
  • C. Readily retrievable filing is the option for Schedule III-V records; it does not extend to Schedule II records, which need their own file.
  • D. Two years is the retention period for controlled substance records, but meeting it does not satisfy the separate-file rule for Schedule II.

Federal rules split controlled substance records by schedule. Schedule II inventories and records, including Form 222 copies and the invoices that go with them, are kept in their own file, separate from the pharmacy's other records, and paper Schedule II prescriptions have their own prescription file. Schedule III-V records may be kept separately or with ordinary business records, as long as they are readily retrievable: they can be separated out in a reasonable time or are marked so they stand out, such as by a flag or red line. These records are kept for at least two years.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1304 (controlled substance records and inventories); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022)

Question 6

An independent community pharmacy now and then sells small amounts of controlled substances to a nearby pharmacy that has run short, receiving a Form 222 from the buyer for each Schedule II sale. Under federal rules, which condition lets the selling pharmacy do this without registering as a distributor?

  1. Its Schedule II sales stay under 10% of the Schedule II units it dispenses each month
  2. The two pharmacies share an owner, so each sale counts as a stock transfer
  3. These sales total at most 5% of its controlled dosage units each calendar year
  4. Each sale is no more than 5% of that drug's stock on hand on the day of the sale

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. There is no separate 10% or monthly Schedule II limit; one 5% calendar-year limit covers the schedules together, and Schedule II sales also need the buyer's Form 222.
  • B. Common ownership does not turn a sale into an internal move; each registered location is its own registrant, so the 5% limit and the Form 222 still apply.
  • C. Correct. Units distributed to other practitioners in a calendar year may not exceed 5% of the total controlled dosage units the pharmacy distributes and dispenses that year (21 CFR 1307.11(a)(1)(iv)).
  • D. The 5% figure is right, but it is measured against the pharmacy's yearly total of controlled dosage units, not the stock of one drug on the day of a sale.

A pharmacy registered to dispense may distribute controlled substances to another practitioner without a distributor registration under the rule often called the five percent rule. The buyer must be registered to dispense the drug, both pharmacies record the transfer, and for Schedule II the buying pharmacy issues a Form 222 or CSOS order to the seller. The total dosage units of controlled substances distributed this way in a calendar year may not exceed 5% of all controlled dosage units the pharmacy distributes and dispenses that year; a pharmacy that expects to go over must register as a distributor, because beyond that point it is acting as a wholesaler rather than a dispenser.

Sources: DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); 21 CFR Part 1305 (DEA Form 222 and CSOS orders)

Question 7

A technician at the certified central pharmacy that ships Xywav (calcium, magnesium, potassium and sodium oxybates) takes a call from a patient who says a bottle was spilled and asks for an early refill shipment. Under the Xywav and Xyrem REMS, how is this request handled?

  1. Ship a replacement overnight, since a spill is an accepted reason
  2. Ask the patient to file a DEA Form 106 for the spilled bottle first
  3. Route it to the pharmacist, who discusses it with the prescriber
  4. Ship the refill once the patient signs and returns a spill statement

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because the REMS pharmacy does ship by overnight service, but reports of spilled drug and early-refill requests are treated as possible signs of misuse or diversion and are reviewed first.
  • B. Tempting because Form 106 reports controlled substance losses, but it is filed by DEA registrants, not by patients, and it does not authorize an early refill.
  • C. Correct. The REMS requires the pharmacy to discuss an early-refill request, or any suspected abuse, misuse or diversion, with the prescriber before shipping more drug.
  • D. Tempting because written statements feel like documentation, but no patient statement replaces the pharmacy's discussion with the prescriber that the REMS requires.

Xywav is a Schedule III oxybate whose active moiety, GHB, is abused, so it is available only through a restricted REMS. Prescribers are certified, patients are enrolled, and a specially certified central pharmacy dispenses it. That pharmacy checks enrollment and overlapping oxybate prescriptions, screens for sedative hypnotics, ships by overnight service, and dispenses no more than a 30-day supply at first and 90 days thereafter. An early refill or spill report is a diversion signal, so the pharmacist reviews it with the prescriber before any shipment.

Sources: FDA: Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 8

A newly hired technician at a mail-order pharmacy will count Schedule II tablets, check in controlled substance deliveries and restock the vault. She asks whether she needs a DEA registration of her own. Under federal law, which answer is correct?

  1. Yes, through a listing on the pharmacy's next Form 224a renewal
  2. Yes, if she will also sign DEA Form 222 orders for the pharmacy
  3. No, she acts under the pharmacy's registration as its employee
  4. Yes, each person who handles Schedule II stock must register

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because Form 224a is the pharmacy's renewal form, but it renews the pharmacy's own registration and is not used to list or register individual employees.
  • B. Tempting because signing order forms is a restricted task, but an employee signs DEA Forms 222 under a power of attorney from the registrant (21 CFR 1305.05), not under a registration of her own.
  • C. Correct. Under 21 CFR 1301.22(a), the registration requirement is waived for an agent or employee of a registrant acting in the usual course of business or employment, so technicians work under the pharmacy's DEA registration.
  • D. Tempting because every pharmacy that dispenses controlled substances must be registered, but the requirement falls on the registered pharmacy at each location, not on each employee.

DEA registers the pharmacy, not its staff. Each pharmacy location that dispenses controlled substances holds its own registration (Form 224 to apply, Form 224a every three years to renew), and 21 CFR 1301.22(a) waives registration for agents and employees acting in the usual course of their employment. The pharmacy stays responsible for the controlled substances its employees handle, and it may grant a power of attorney so that a trusted employee can sign DEA Forms 222. State boards of pharmacy may separately license or register technicians, which is a state requirement rather than a DEA one.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1301 (DEA registration and security); 21 CFR Part 1305 (DEA Form 222 and CSOS orders); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022)

Question 9

A patient brings a new prescription for phentermine 37.5 mg tablets to a community pharmacy. The technician notes that the label describes phentermine as chemically related to the amphetamines. Under federal law, which of these drugs is in the same schedule as phentermine?

  1. Lisdexamfetamine capsules
  2. Ketamine injection
  3. Temazepam capsules
  4. Pregabalin capsules

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because phentermine is related to the amphetamines and lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) is an amphetamine prodrug, but lisdexamfetamine is Schedule II.
  • B. Tempting because ketamine is a non-narcotic controlled drug like phentermine, but ketamine is Schedule III.
  • C. Correct. Phentermine and temazepam are both Schedule IV, as their DailyMed labels state; Schedule IV also holds the other benzodiazepines, tramadol and modafinil.
  • D. Tempting because pregabalin is a lower-schedule controlled drug, but it is Schedule V, one step below phentermine.

Phentermine is a Schedule IV stimulant used short term for weight loss. Its structural link to amphetamine makes Schedule II a natural guess, but DEA placed it with drugs of lower abuse potential, alongside temazepam, alprazolam, lorazepam, tramadol and modafinil. The schedule tells the technician which rules apply: a Schedule IV prescription may be refilled up to five times within six months of the date written and may arrive on paper, by fax from the prescriber, electronically or orally to the pharmacist, whereas Schedule II amphetamines cannot be refilled. The phentermine label also calls for a tight, child-resistant container.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); 21 CFR Part 1308 (schedules of controlled substances)

Question 10

A technician at a long-term care pharmacy builds the controlled-substance count sheets, which are grouped by federal schedule. Which pair of drugs belongs on the same count sheet?

  1. Zolpidem and lacosamide
  2. Lorazepam and ketamine
  3. Lisdexamfetamine and buprenorphine
  4. Tramadol and alprazolam

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Zolpidem is Schedule IV, but lacosamide, an anticonvulsant, is Schedule V, one schedule lower.
  • B. Lorazepam is Schedule IV, but ketamine is Schedule III, so the pair splits across two sheets.
  • C. Both carry a high diversion risk, which suggests one schedule, but lisdexamfetamine is Schedule II and buprenorphine is Schedule III.
  • D. Correct. Tramadol and alprazolam are both Schedule IV, so they share a sheet.

Tramadol is a Schedule IV narcotic (21 CFR 1308.14(b)), the example the DEA Pharmacist's Manual gives for that schedule, and alprazolam sits in Schedule IV with the other benzodiazepines such as lorazepam and clonazepam. Buprenorphine and ketamine are Schedule III, lisdexamfetamine is a Schedule II stimulant, and lacosamide is one of the Schedule V anticonvulsants with pregabalin and brivaracetam. Opioid does not mean Schedule II, which is why tramadol and buprenorphine are easy to misplace: the schedule follows the DEA listing, not the drug class, so check the label symbol when unsure.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 1308 (schedules of controlled substances); DEA Pharmacist's Manual (2022); Tramadol hydrochloride tablets label (Amneal, 2021)
