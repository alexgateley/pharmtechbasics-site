Question 1 · Role and General Duties

At a community pharmacy, a technician has counted, labeled and bagged a refill of Zoloft 100 mg tablets. Before checking it, the pharmacist stepped into the consultation room with another patient. The patient arrives and says she is double-parked. The store has no technician-check program. What should the technician do?

  1. Release it because the dose and directions match last month's fill
  2. Have the second technician on duty check it, then release it
  3. Release it after the stock bottle scan matches the label
  4. Keep it behind the counter until the pharmacist completes the final check

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A refill that looks unchanged can still hold a counting, labeling or product error, and the profile may have changed since last month. Similarity to an earlier fill does not replace this fill's verification.
  • B. A second set of technician eyes can catch errors, but without an approved technician-check program it does not replace the pharmacist's final verification.
  • C. A barcode match confirms the product and strength the technician pulled. It is a filling safeguard, not the pharmacist's review of the prescription, the profile and the finished product.
  • D. Correct. The technician tells the patient the pharmacist will check it shortly and keeps the bag behind the counter until the pharmacist has verified it.

The NHA test plan lists making sure the pharmacist has completed final verification before a prescription is released to the patient. Federal OBRA 90 rules for Medicaid add that the pharmacist screens each prescription for drug problems and offers counseling before it is dispensed, and pharmacies apply that review to every patient. The technician's part is to keep the patient informed, give an honest wait time and let the pharmacist know a patient is waiting. Scans and peer checks help catch errors, but they are steps within filling, not the pharmacist's check.

Sources: NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis); 42 CFR 456 Subpart K (OBRA 90 drug use review)

Question 2 · Role and General Duties

A mail-order pharmacy receives a recall notice for one lot of metoprolol succinate ER 50 mg tablets in 500-count bottles. The notice sets the depth of the recall at the retail level and asks pharmacies to stop dispensing the lot and return remaining stock. FDA has not yet posted a class. Which action does this notice require of the pharmacy?

  1. Call each patient shipped the lot and arrange to replace it
  2. Destroy the remaining tablets on site and record the count
  3. Hold the lot on the shelf until FDA posts the recall class
  4. Pull and quarantine the lot, then return it as the notice directs

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Reaching patients is what a consumer- or user-level recall asks for; this notice stops at the retail level, and any decision to contact patients beyond it belongs to the pharmacist.
  • B. The notice asks for the stock to be returned, and the firm uses returned quantities to account for the lot; destroying it on site skips that step.
  • C. The firm's instructions apply as soon as the notice arrives; waiting for the class keeps a recalled lot in dispensing stock.
  • D. Correct. A retail-level recall reaches the pharmacy's own stock, so the lot is pulled from inventory, kept apart from dispensable stock and returned as the firm instructs.

Every recall strategy states its depth: the wholesale level, the retail level, or the consumer or user level. A retail-level recall reaches pharmacies and other retailers, so the pharmacy stops dispensing the lot, moves it out of active inventory, labels it as recalled, and returns it as the firm's notice directs. A consumer-level recall goes further and reaches patients who already received the product. Under 21 CFR 7.49, a recipient carries out the notice's instructions immediately, without waiting for FDA to post the class.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 7, Subpart C (recalls); FDA: Recalls Background and Definitions; DailyMed: Metoprolol Succinate ER Tablets, Ascend (2026)

Question 3 · Laws and Regulations

While checking in a wholesaler order, a technician finds two bottles of the same codeine-guaifenesin oral solution from the same labeler: NDC 58657-500-04 and NDC 58657-500-16. Under the National Drug Code system that federal drug listing law requires, what does the difference in the final segment tell the technician?

  1. The strength or formulation differs
  2. The labeler or repackager differs
  3. The package size or type differs
  4. The manufacturing lot differs

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because strength is what a technician checks first, but strength, dosage form and formulation are coded in the middle (product) segment, which is 500 on both bottles.
  • B. Tempting because repackaged stock carries a different NDC, but the labeler is the first segment, assigned by FDA, and 58657 is the same on both bottles.
  • C. Correct. The third segment is the package code, which the firm assigns to each package size and type; on this label -04 is a 120 mL bottle and -16 a 473 mL bottle.
  • D. Tempting because lots also vary between bottles, but the lot number and expiration date are printed separately and are not part of the NDC.

The Drug Listing Act requires every registered establishment to list each drug it makes or repackages for commercial distribution with FDA, and each listed drug carries a three-segment NDC. FDA assigns the labeler code; the firm assigns the product code (strength, dosage form, formulation) and the package code (package size and type). A changed last segment therefore means the same drug and labeler in a different package, which matters when matching an order, billing the right package or pulling a recall. An NDC does not mean FDA approval, and FDA has adopted a uniform 12-digit NDC format that takes effect March 7, 2033.

Sources: FDA National Drug Code Directory; 21 CFR Part 207 (drug listing and the NDC); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 4 · Laws and Regulations

A hospital pharmacy operating under the EPA's Subpart P rule has two outgoing shipments: a sealed box of potentially creditable hazardous waste pharmaceuticals for its reverse distributor, and a full container of non-creditable hazardous waste pharmaceuticals. Under federal EPA rules, which shipment must travel on a hazardous waste manifest?

  1. The reverse distributor box, as it holds hazardous drugs
  2. The non-creditable container, bound for a disposal facility
  3. Both shipments, each one marked PHRM on its manifest
  4. Neither, since Subpart P uses a 35-day delivery confirmation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The box does hold hazardous waste pharmaceuticals, which is why it tempts, but the rule says potentially creditable items do not need a manifest; they ship under DOT rules with delivery confirmation instead.
  • B. Correct. Non-creditable hazardous waste pharmaceuticals ship to a designated facility, such as a permitted treatment or disposal site, on a hazardous waste manifest with PHRM or PHARMS written in Item 13.
  • C. PHRM is a real marking, but it belongs on the manifest for the non-creditable container; the reverse distributor box travels without a manifest.
  • D. Delivery confirmation within 35 days is the check for potentially creditable shipments only; the non-creditable container still needs a manifest.

Subpart P splits a pharmacy's hazardous waste pharmaceuticals into two streams. Non-creditable waste (40 CFR 266.508) is packaged, labeled and marked under DOT hazardous materials rules and shipped to a designated disposal facility on the Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest, where the facility must write PHRM or PHARMS in Item 13 and need not list every waste code. Potentially creditable waste (266.509) goes to a reverse distributor without a manifest; the reverse distributor confirms receipt, and if no confirmation arrives within 35 calendar days the pharmacy must contact the carrier and the reverse distributor. Packing the right papers with each box keeps both streams traceable.

Sources: EPA: Management of Hazardous Waste Pharmaceuticals

Question 5 · Drugs and Drug Therapy

At a mail-order pharmacy, a technician enters a new prescription for Coreg CR 20 mg, one by mouth every morning with food, with generic substitution permitted. Which product should the technician select?

  1. Metoprolol succinate ER 25 mg tablets
  2. Carvedilol phosphate ER 20 mg capsules
  3. Carvedilol 6.25 mg tablets taken twice daily
  4. Carvedilol 20 mg immediate-release tablets, once daily

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Metoprolol succinate is another once-daily extended-release beta blocker, which makes it feel similar, but it is a different drug.
  • B. Correct. Coreg CR is carvedilol phosphate in extended-release capsules of 10, 20, 40 and 80 mg, taken once daily.
  • C. The label's conversion table pairs 6.25 mg twice daily with 20 mg of Coreg CR, but changing the dosage form and schedule is a prescriber decision, not a substitution.
  • D. Immediate-release carvedilol (Coreg) comes as 3.125, 6.25, 12.5 and 25 mg tablets taken twice daily, so no 20 mg tablet matches this order.

Coreg tablets are immediate-release carvedilol, while Coreg CR is carvedilol phosphate in extended-release capsules taken once a day in the morning with food and swallowed whole (or opened and sprinkled over a spoonful of applesauce that is not warm). Carvedilol blocks both beta receptors and alpha-1 receptors, and the label lists heart failure, left ventricular dysfunction after a heart attack, and hypertension. The Orange Book treats different dosage forms, such as an immediate-release tablet and an extended-release capsule, as pharmaceutical alternatives rather than equivalents, so a technician cannot trade one for the other even when the daily dose matches. Any such change goes to the pharmacist and prescriber.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); FDA Orange Book

Question 6 · Drugs and Drug Therapy

At a mail-order pharmacy, a technician processes a 90-day prescription for Cymbalta 60 mg, one capsule daily. Which generic product matches this brand?

  1. Doxepin hydrochloride capsules
  2. Desvenlafaxine succinate tablets
  3. Duloxetine delayed-release capsules
  4. Fluvoxamine maleate tablets

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Doxepin is a capsule antidepressant that also starts with 'd', but it is a tricyclic, not duloxetine.
  • B. Desvenlafaxine (Pristiq) is also an SNRI taken once daily, which makes it the closest trap, but it is a different drug in an extended-release tablet.
  • C. Correct. Cymbalta is duloxetine hydrochloride in delayed-release capsules of 20, 30 and 60 mg, a serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor.
  • D. Fluvoxamine is a serotonin reuptake inhibitor, which sounds close to an SNRI, but it is an SSRI labeled in the US for obsessive-compulsive disorder and is not the drug in Cymbalta.

Cymbalta is duloxetine hydrochloride, an SNRI supplied as 20, 30 and 60 mg delayed-release capsules. The label says to swallow the capsule whole and not crush, chew or open it, because opening it or sprinkling the contents on food may affect the enteric coating. The label carries a boxed warning for suicidal thoughts and behaviors and comes with a Medication Guide, which goes out with every mail-order fill, including a 90-day supply. Desvenlafaxine and duloxetine are both once-daily SNRIs, so the brand alone settles which one is dispensed.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 7 · Dispensing Process

A hospital pharmacy is reorganizing its storage under USP <800>. Which product must be stored apart from non-hazardous drugs in an externally vented, negative-pressure room with at least 12 air changes per hour?

  1. Warfarin tablets kept for the inpatient unit-dose cart
  2. Estradiol patches dispensed in the manufacturer's sealed pouches
  3. Tamoxifen tablets that are counted for outpatient prescriptions
  4. Fluorouracil vials stocked for IV room compounding

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Warfarin is on Table 2 of the NIOSH list, and its handling, storage included, is set by the facility's assessment of risk rather than this storage rule.
  • B. Estradiol is a Table 2 hazardous drug, and a sealed patch that is dispensed unchanged can be handled under an assessment of risk.
  • C. Tamoxifen is an antineoplastic on Table 1, but a final dosage form that is only counted may be stored with other stock if the facility's policy allows it.
  • D. Correct. Fluorouracil is an antineoplastic that is manipulated during compounding, so it is stored separately in a negative-pressure, externally vented room.

USP <800> requires antineoplastic hazardous drugs that need manipulation beyond counting or repackaging of final dosage forms, and any hazardous active pharmaceutical ingredient, to be stored apart from non-hazardous drugs in an externally ventilated room under negative pressure with at least 12 air changes per hour. The room keeps vapour or residue from a broken vial from reaching other stock and staff, and refrigerated antineoplastics go in a dedicated refrigerator in a negative-pressure area. Tablets and capsules that are only counted, and Table 2 drugs, may be stored with other inventory when the facility's documented assessment of risk allows it.

Sources: USP General Chapters <795>, <797> and <800>; NIOSH List of Hazardous Drugs in Healthcare Settings, 2024

Question 8 · Dispensing Process

An IV room technician receives an order for 500 mL of dextrose 20% and sets up an alligation grid with the D10W and D5W bags on the shelf, but the grid gives no usable answer. The shelf also holds D50W vials and sterile water for injection. Which change lets the technician prepare the order?

  1. Replace the D5W with sterile water
  2. Replace the D5W with D50W
  3. Mix the D10W and D5W in equal volumes
  4. Use more D10W than D5W in the mix

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because sterile water is a common diluent, but water is 0%, so D10W and water can make strengths below 10% and nothing higher.
  • B. Correct. D50W and D10W bracket 20%, giving 10 parts of D50W to 30 parts of D10W: 125 mL of D50W and 375 mL of D10W.
  • C. Tempting as a quick fix, but equal volumes of D10W and D5W give 7.5%, the midpoint of the two and well short of 20%.
  • D. Tempting because D10W is the stronger stock, but even D10W by itself is 10%, so changing the proportions cannot climb above it.

A mixture of two stocks can land anywhere between their strengths but never outside them, so alligation needs one stock stronger and one weaker than the desired strength. D10W and D5W both sit below 20%, which is why the grid fails. Swapping in D50W fixes it: parts of D50W = 20 − 10 = 10 and parts of D10W = 50 − 20 = 30, a 1-to-3 ratio, so 500 mL × 10 ÷ 40 = 125 mL of D50W and 375 mL of D10W. Check: 62.5 g + 37.5 g = 100 g in 500 mL, or 20%. D50W with sterile water would also bracket 20%. The Hospira label lists D50W as 25 g/50 mL (500 mg/mL), and the Baxter label lists D10W in 250, 500 and 1,000 mL containers.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis)

Question 9 · Dispensing Process

At month end, a pharmacy manager asks a technician to prepare gross profit and net profit figures for prescription sales. Which amount is subtracted when finding net profit but is left out when finding gross profit?

  1. Copays collected from patients at pickup
  2. Wages for staff, store rent and monthly utilities
  3. Acquisition cost of the drugs dispensed
  4. Dispensing fees paid by third-party plans

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because copays move through the register separately, but they are part of the revenue for each prescription and are added in, not subtracted.
  • B. Correct. These operating (overhead) costs are what separate net profit from gross profit; gross profit stops at revenue minus the cost of the drugs.
  • C. Tempting because it is the biggest cost on the report, but it is subtracted in both calculations: it is the step that produces gross profit in the first place.
  • D. Tempting because the word fee sounds like a cost, but a plan's dispensing fee is money paid to the pharmacy, so it is revenue in both calculations.

Gross profit is total prescription revenue (plan payments plus copays, including any dispensing fees) minus the cost of the drugs dispensed. Net profit takes gross profit and subtracts the costs of running the pharmacy: wages, rent, utilities, insurance, supplies, software and delivery. Because those overhead costs are large, a pharmacy can show a strong gross profit and a thin or negative net profit, which is why both figures appear on a monthly report and why reducing waste and dispensing time matters to the bottom line.

Sources: NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis)

Question 10 · Dispensing Process

At a community pharmacy, a new prescription for Ozempic (semaglutide) 2 mg/3 mL pen, inject 0.25 mg once weekly, rejects from the patient's commercial plan with the message 'Prior authorization required.' What should the technician do NEXT?

  1. Resubmit the claim with a prior authorization override code
  2. Notify the prescriber's office that the plan needs a prior authorization
  3. Fill the GLP-1 agonist that the plan lists as preferred
  4. Fill out the plan's request using the diagnosis the patient gives

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Override codes answer certain plan edits after review, but a prior authorization requirement is lifted only when the plan approves the request, so resubmitting returns the same rejection.
  • B. Correct. The prescriber sends the plan the clinical information it asks for, and the technician lets the patient know about the likely delay.
  • C. A different active ingredient is a therapeutic substitution, which needs a new order from the prescriber after the pharmacist reviews it.
  • D. The plan wants the prescriber's clinical record, such as the diagnosis and earlier therapy, and a patient's account cannot stand in for it.

A prior authorization rejection means the plan will pay only after it reviews the prescriber's reasons for the drug. The pharmacy's part is clerical: tell the prescriber's office, often by the plan's fax or electronic form, note the rejection on the prescription, and tell the patient that the fill is waiting on the plan. Once the plan approves, the claim is resubmitted and pays. If the patient asks for another drug or the cash price, the technician can quote the price but brings any drug change to the pharmacist.

Sources: NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis); OZEMPIC (semaglutide) injection label (Novo Nordisk, 2026)

Question 11 · Dispensing Process

A technician in a hospital IV room is compounding in a horizontal laminar airflow workbench, where HEPA-filtered air blows from the back of the hood toward the technician. Where should the technician's hands be while adding a drug through the injection port of an IV bag?

  1. Beside or downstream of the port
  2. Upstream of the port, in freshly sanitized gloves
  3. Between the HEPA filter and the port, shielding it
  4. Within 3 inches of the hood opening, for easy reach

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Hands placed beside or in front of the port leave the HEPA-filtered first air free to reach it.
  • B. Sanitizing gloves with sterile 70% IPA lowers their bioburden, but gloved hands upstream still block first air and shed particles onto the port.
  • C. Hands upstream of the port block first air, and the air that then reaches the port has washed over skin and gloves.
  • D. Air near the front opening mixes with room air, which is why work is done at least 6 inches inside the hood rather than at its edge.

First air is the particle-free air that leaves the HEPA filter in a unidirectional stream. In a horizontal hood it travels from the back of the work surface toward the technician, so anything placed between the filter and a critical site, such as a needle hub, vial septum or injection port, turns that clean air into air that has swept over a contaminated surface. Smoke studies show turbulence under the palm when hands are held near critical points. The technician works at least 6 inches inside the hood, keeps supplies from blocking one another, and keeps hands beside or downstream of critical sites.

Sources: USP General Chapters <795>, <797> and <800>; NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis)

Question 12 · Dispensing Process

A mail-order pharmacy technician is preparing a shipment of Symbicort (budesonide and formoterol fumarate dihydrate) 160/4.5 mcg inhalation aerosol, two inhalations twice daily. Which auxiliary label matches the product labeling?

  1. Use two inhalations as needed for sudden wheezing
  2. Rinse mouth with water and spit after each use
  3. Store the inhaler upright with the mouthpiece on top
  4. Prime with two test sprays before each scheduled dose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because formoterol is a bronchodilator, but the label states Symbicort is not indicated for the relief of acute bronchospasm; it is a scheduled twice-daily maintenance inhaler.
  • B. Correct. The Symbicort label says the patient should rinse the mouth with water without swallowing after inhalation, which lowers the risk of oral Candida infection from the inhaled corticosteroid.
  • C. Tempting because upright storage sounds tidy, but the label says to store the inhaler with the mouthpiece down.
  • D. Tempting because priming is in the label, but two test sprays are released before first use, after 7 days without use or after a drop, not before every dose, which would waste medicine.

Symbicort combines budesonide, an inhaled corticosteroid, with formoterol, a long-acting beta2-agonist. Because the steroid can leave residue in the mouth and throat, the label directs rinsing with water without swallowing after each use. The canister is shaken for 5 seconds before each spray, kept at controlled room temperature with the mouthpiece down, and primed with two sprays before first use or after 7 days of non-use. A patient asking how to use it for an attack is referred to the pharmacist.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 13 · Dispensing Process

A community pharmacy receives an e-prescription for omeprazole 20 mg delayed-release capsules as part of H. pylori triple therapy with amoxicillin and clarithromycin: take 1 capsule by mouth twice daily for 10 days, then 1 capsule once daily for 18 days. How many omeprazole capsules cover the full course?

  1. 38 capsules
  2. 28 capsules
  3. 56 capsules
  4. 48 capsules

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The twice-daily phase uses 2 × 10 = 20 capsules and the once-daily phase uses 1 × 18 = 18 capsules, for 38 capsules over 28 days.
  • B. This is one capsule a day for all 28 days, which misses the second daily capsule during the 10 days of triple therapy.
  • C. This is twice daily for all 28 days; the twice-daily dose runs only through the 10 days the antibiotics are taken.
  • D. This adds 28 once-daily capsules to the 20 of the first phase, counting the whole 28-day course again instead of the 18 days that follow.

Two-phase sigs are counted phase by phase and then added. In the omeprazole label's H. pylori triple-therapy regimen, omeprazole 20 mg, amoxicillin 1000 mg and clarithromycin 500 mg are each taken twice daily for 10 days, and if an ulcer is present omeprazole 20 mg continues once daily for another 18 days. That gives 20 + 18 = 38 capsules and a 28-day supply. The two antibiotics are separate prescriptions with a 10-day supply each, so their entries and refill dates differ from the omeprazole entry.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 14 · Medication Safety and QA

A community pharmacist is reporting a patient's serious reaction to FDA on Form FDA 3500 and asks the technician to fill in the patient identifier box in Section A. The patient is a woman whose profile is in the pharmacy system. What should the technician enter?

  1. Leave it blank, since MedWatch reports are anonymous
  2. Her Social Security number for a unique match
  3. Her initials or a pharmacy patient number
  4. Her full name, so FDA can follow up with her

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because FDA protects identities, but the reporter is named on the form and the box is filled in; FDA enters 'none' only when no patient was involved.
  • B. Tempting because it is unique to her, but the instructions rule it out alongside the name; a pharmacy patient number gives a unique match without exposing it.
  • C. Correct. FDA asks for initials, a patient number or another identifier that lets the reporter find the case if FDA follows up.
  • D. Tempting because a name seems the clearest identifier, but FDA's instructions say not to use the patient's name; FDA follows up with the reporter, not the patient.

FDA's instructions for Form 3500 ask for the patient's initials, a patient number or some other identifier that will let the reporter locate the case if FDA asks for more information, and they say not to use the patient's name or Social Security number. FDA holds the patient's identity in strict confidence and will not release the reporter's identity to the public under the Freedom of Information Act, although it may share the reporter's identity with the manufacturer unless the reporter asks it not to. Using the pharmacy's own patient number keeps the link to the record without sending a name.

Sources: FDA MedWatch; PTCB PTCE Content Outline (effective January 6, 2026)

Question 15 · Medication Safety and QA

At a community pharmacy, a prescription is written for cefuroxime axetil 500 mg tablets, 1 tablet by mouth every 12 hours for 10 days, #20. The vial label is correct, but the technician counted cefprozil 500 mg tablets into it from the shelf next to the cefuroxime. Which type of prescription error is this?

  1. Incorrect drug
  2. Incorrect dosage form
  3. Incorrect strength
  4. An allowed generic substitution

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Cefprozil and cefuroxime axetil are different active ingredients, so the vial holds a drug other than the one prescribed and labeled, even though the strength and count match.
  • B. Tempting because cefuroxime axetil is also sold as an oral suspension, but both products here are oral tablets, so the dosage form is not the problem.
  • C. Tempting because strength mix-ups are a common picking error, but both products are 500 mg tablets, so the strength matches; the active ingredient is what differs.
  • D. Tempting because both are generic cephalosporins, but a generic substitute must contain the same active ingredient in the same strength and form; swapping to another drug in the class is therapeutic substitution, which needs the prescriber's approval through the pharmacist.

Prescription errors are classed by what went wrong: the patient, the drug, the strength or dose, the dosage form, the route, the quantity or the directions. Cefuroxime axetil and cefprozil are both oral cephalosporins sold as 250 mg and 500 mg tablets, and their similar names and neighbouring shelf spots make them easy to confuse, but they are different molecules with different dosing schedules (cefprozil is given every 12 or 24 hours depending on the infection). In the Orange Book, a generic substitute is a pharmaceutical equivalent: identical active ingredient, amount, dosage form and route. Scanning the stock bottle's barcode against the prescription during filling catches this kind of pick before the pharmacist's check.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); FDA Orange Book; PTCB PTCE Content Outline (effective January 6, 2026)
