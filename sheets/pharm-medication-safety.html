Look-alike, sound-alike names

Drug names that look or sound alike cause wrong-drug errors. Tall Man lettering puts the letters that differ in capitals so the eye stops on them. FDA's Name Differentiation Project (since 2001) asks makers to use it on labels; ISMP publishes a longer list.

  • glipiZIDE and glyBURIDE
  • buPROPion and busPIRone
  • predniSONE and prednisoLONE
  • traZODone and traMADol
  • DOBUTamine and DOPamine
Exam tip Tall Man letters highlight the part of the name that differs; they do not change the drug. Exams also test the other fixes: shelve look-alikes apart, use shelf alerts, and scan.

High-alert medications

ISMP calls a drug high-alert when an error with it is more likely to cause serious harm. Errors may be no more common; they are more dangerous. Main groups: insulin, opioids, anticoagulants, concentrated electrolytes (hospital), oral chemotherapy, and oral methotrexate for non-cancer use.

Safety Methotrexate taken daily instead of weekly has killed patients (boxed warning). U-500 insulin is five times as concentrated as U-100. Check these twice and bring every question to the pharmacist.

The Joint Commission keeps a short do-not-use list; ISMP keeps a longer list of error-prone abbreviations, symbols and dose designations. If an order uses one, do not guess: the pharmacist clarifies it.

Safeguards in the workflow

  • Two identifiers, such as full name and date of birth, at drop-off, at entry and at pickup
  • Scan the stock bottle's bar code (NDC) against the entry for every fill; stop at any mismatch
  • Separate look-alike stock on the shelf; use Tall Man shelf labels and alerts
  • Keep the original order, stock bottle and filled vial together for the pharmacist's final check
  • At pickup, open the bag with the patient and confirm each drug name

What counts as a medication error

NCC MERP, whose definition FDA uses, calls a medication error any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm while the medication is in the control of a health professional, patient or consumer. It covers every step: prescribing, order communication, labeling, packaging, naming, compounding, dispensing, distribution, administration, education, monitoring and use.

TermMeaning
Medication errorA preventable mistake at any step. It may or may not harm the patient.
Adverse drug event (ADE)Harm to a patient from a medication. It does not need an error.
Preventable ADEHarm caused by an error that reached the patient
Adverse drug reactionHarm from a drug used as intended; not caused by an error
Near miss (close call)An error caught before it reached the patient, or that did no harm only by luck

The NCC MERP Index sorts errors by whether they reached the patient and how much harm they caused.

CategoryWhat happenedGroup
ACircumstances that could cause an errorNo error
BError did not reach the patientError, no harm
CReached the patient, no harmError, no harm
DReached the patient; monitoring or intervention needed to make sure there was no harmError, no harm
ETemporary harm needing interventionError, harm
FTemporary harm needing initial or longer hospitalizationError, harm
GPermanent harmError, harm
HIntervention needed to sustain lifeError, harm
IMay have contributed to or caused deathError, death
Exam tip An error of omission (a dose never given) counts as reaching the patient. A wrong drug caught at the final check is Category B, a near miss.

Tall Man lettering and look-alike stock

FDA started the Name Differentiation Project in 2001 to keep reviewing reports of confusion between similar established (generic) names. FDA weighs how alike the names look, how much harm a mix-up could cause, whether the products overlap in strength, form or use, and the error reports. When Tall Man lettering fits, FDA asks manufacturers to revise their labels voluntarily. The method capitalizes the letters that differ, working from the left side of the name, and can be combined with bold or color.

FDA Tall Man pairWhy a mix-up matters
hydrALAZINE / hydrOXYzineBlood pressure drug versus antihistamine
glipiZIDE / glyBURIDETwo different sulfonylureas for diabetes
predniSONE / prednisoLONETwo different corticosteroids
risperiDONE / rOPINIRoleAntipsychotic versus Parkinson's and restless legs drug
traZODone / traMADolAntidepressant versus opioid pain reliever
vinBLAStine / vinCRIStineTwo chemotherapy drugs, not interchangeable

ISMP's List of Confused Drug Names (2024) is longer. It gathers look-alike and sound-alike pairs from ISMP's newsletters and from both the FDA and ISMP Tall Man lists. ISMP also suggests putting both the brand and generic names and the purpose of the drug on prescriptions and labels, and making computer searches need at least the first five letters of a name. FDA reviews proposed brand names before approval to screen out names too close to existing ones, and has changed a marketed brand name for this reason (Brintellix, renamed because it was confused with Brilinta).

  • Use Tall Man names on shelf labels, bin tags and computer pick lists, not just on the manufacturer's label
  • Do not shelve look-alike pairs side by side; separate them, or add a shelf alert where they must sit close
  • Keep different strengths of the same high-alert drug apart
  • Read the whole name and the strength on the stock bottle, not just the first letters
  • When a product looks new or different, ask before filling; a changed manufacturer can mean a changed tablet
Safety Tall Man lettering helps the eye; it does not replace scanning. Sound-alike errors start with spoken orders: read back and spell drug names.

High-alert medications in more detail

ISMP keeps separate high-alert lists for acute care (2024), community and ambulatory care (2021) and long-term care. They are copyrighted; use them from ISMP. The idea behind them: mistakes with these drugs 'may or may not be more common', but the harm when they happen is far worse, so they get extra safeguards.

GroupExample of the danger
InsulinU-500 is five times as concentrated as U-100; the label says to use only a U-500 syringe, and the U-500 pen dials 5 units per click
OpioidsToo much can slow or stop breathing
AnticoagulantsToo much causes bleeding; too little allows clots
Oral methotrexate (non-cancer use)Usually once weekly; taken daily by mistake it has caused deaths
Concentrated electrolytes (hospital)Concentrated potassium chloride must be diluted before it is given
Oral chemotherapyComplex cycles of days on and off; also a hazardous drug to handle
  • Limit the strengths and package sizes stocked
  • Use auxiliary labels and computer alerts, for example 'Take once a week' with the day written in
  • Use an independent double check where the pharmacy's policy calls for it
  • Make sure the pharmacist counsels every new high-alert prescription; the technician does not explain how to take it

Abbreviations and dose designations

The Joint Commission's official do-not-use list is short and applies to the organizations it accredits: they must include its items on their own do-not-use list. It covers U (write unit), IU (write International Unit), QD (write daily), QOD (write every other day), a trailing zero, a missing leading zero, and MS, MSO4 and MgSO4 (write morphine sulfate or magnesium sulfate). ISMP's List of Error-Prone Abbreviations, Symbols and Dose Designations (2024) is much longer and marks the Joint Commission items. ISMP says the items on it should never be used, in speech, in electronic systems or by hand. Both lists are copyrighted; read them from the source.

  • Leading zero: always write 0 before a decimal under one (0.5 mg, 0.25 mL)
  • Trailing zero: never end a dose with a zero after the decimal point (5 mg, not 5.0 mg; 2.5 mL, not 2.50 mL)
  • Leave a space between the dose and the unit (5 mL, not 5mL) and between the drug name and the dose
  • Write mcg, not the Greek letter symbol, which can be read as mg (a 1000-fold error)
  • Spell out daily, every other day, units and subcutaneously

NCPDP's 2021 recommendations for oral liquids: label doses in mL only, with leading zeros and no trailing zeros, and dispense a dosing device marked in mL that matches the label, such as an oral syringe for doses up to 10 mL.

Exam tip A missing leading zero or an added trailing zero causes a tenfold error. Expect a question that asks which dose is written correctly.

Bar codes and what scanning catches

Federal rule 21 CFR 201.25 requires a linear bar code containing at least the NDC on most prescription drugs and on OTC drugs that are dispensed under an order and commonly used in hospitals. Hospitals scan it at the bedside. Pharmacies scan the same code on the stock bottle during filling to check that the product matches the one entered. A final rule published March 5, 2026 will move every NDC to one 12-digit format and allow other kinds of bar code; it takes effect March 7, 2033, so today's 10-digit NDCs and linear bar codes remain the rule until then.

Scanning catchesScanning does not catch
Wrong drug picked from the shelfWrong drug entered in the first place
Wrong strength or dosage formWrong directions typed on the label
A different NDC (package or maker) than the one enteredWrong count from the bottle
A look-alike bottle pulled from beside the right oneFilled on the wrong patient profile
Safety Scan the bottle you actually pour from, every time. Scanning a spare label, scanning one bottle for several, or bypassing a mismatch turns off the safeguard.

Drug utilization review and the pharmacist

Federal Medicaid rules (OBRA '90, 42 CFR 456.705) require each state Medicaid program to provide for prospective DUR before each prescription is filled or delivered to a Medicaid patient. The screen looks for:

  • Therapeutic duplication
  • Drug-disease contraindications
  • Drug-drug interactions
  • Incorrect dose or duration
  • Drug-allergy interactions
  • Clinical abuse or misuse

The same rule requires state counseling standards that include an offer to counsel (each state decides whether staff other than the pharmacist may make the offer), and a patient profile with demographics, allergies, drug reactions, diseases and a medication list. The federal rule covers Medicaid patients, and Medicare Part D plans must run a similar DUR check before each prescription is dispensed (42 CFR 423.153). States write counseling and DUR rules into pharmacy law, usually for all patients, and may go further.

The technician's part is good data: the right patient, current allergies (and what the reaction was), other medications including OTC and herbal products, and accurate entry. The pharmacist reviews the alert, contacts the prescriber if needed and decides. Pickup is the last point where a patient's question about side effects or a missed refill can reach the pharmacist.

Reporting systems

SystemDetail
MedWatch (FDA)Voluntary for health professionals and patients; mandatory for industry. Covers drugs, biologics, devices, supplements, infant formula and cosmetics (vaccines go to VAERS). Online, by PDF form, or by phone at 1-888-INFO-FDA (1-888-463-6332), option 2.
Serious outcome (FDA)Death, life-threatening, hospitalization (initial or prolonged), disability or permanent damage, birth defect, intervention to prevent permanent damage (devices), or other important medical event
FAERS / AEMSFAERS is FDA's database of adverse event reports, medication error reports and product quality complaints for drugs and therapeutic biologics; MedWatch reports feed it. FDA is building one Adverse Event Monitoring System (AEMS) to replace its separate reporting systems, and its FAERS page is now titled AEMS, formerly FAERS.
VAERSRun by CDC and FDA since 1990. Anyone can report. Providers must report events on the VAERS Table of Reportable Events and events the maker lists as reasons not to give more doses, and are urged to report any other event and vaccine administration errors; makers must report all events. A report does not prove the vaccine caused it. 1-800-822-7967.
ISMP MERP and VERPConfidential programs for health professionals to report medication errors and vaccine errors; ISMP staff may follow up. ISMP shares lessons in its Medication Safety Alert! newsletters.
Internal reportRequired by the pharmacy's policy and in some states by law; used for the pharmacy's own quality program

Vaccine reports go to VAERS, not MedWatch. Product quality problems (a cracked tablet, the wrong tablet in a sealed bottle, a label error) go to MedWatch and to the pharmacist, who decides whether to quarantine the stock.

Root-cause analysis, quality improvement and just culture

Root-cause analysis (RCA) is a structured review of a serious event. A team gathers the facts, rebuilds the sequence of events, and separates the active error (the slip at the counter) from the latent problems behind it (staffing, shelf layout, look-alike packaging, interruptions, software). The goal is to remove the latent problems. Some now call it RCA2, root cause analysis and action, because a review that produces no real change has not done its job.

Weaker fixStronger fix
Retrain staff, remind them of the policyChange the system: scanning that blocks a mismatch, separate shelving
'Be more careful'Remove the look-alike strength from stock, add a hard stop alert

Continuous quality improvement (CQI) is the ongoing side: collect error and near-miss reports, look for patterns, test a change, measure it and keep what works. The Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle is the usual method: plan a change, try it on a small scale, study the result, then adopt, adapt or drop it.

No federal rule sets a CQI program for every community pharmacy (Medicare Part D plans must have quality assurance systems, and they rely on state pharmacy practice standards); many states require one. California's rule (16 CCR 1711) is one example: the pharmacy starts a review within 2 business days of finding an error, tells the patient and prescriber when the drug was taken or the error caused a clinically significant delay in therapy, keeps the record for at least 1 year and uses the findings to change its systems.

Behavior (just culture)Response
Human error: an unintended slipFix the system that allowed it; no punishment
At-risk behavior: a shortcut taken despite the riskFix the system that made the shortcut tempting
Reckless behavior: knowingly skipping a required safety stepDiscipline, whether or not anyone was harmed
Exam tip In a just culture the response depends on the behavior, not on how bad the outcome was. Staff report errors and near misses because they will not be blamed for honest mistakes.
