Try one

DEA Schedules, Forms and Controlled Prescriptions practice question

A long-time patient at a community pharmacy brings back her empty amber plastic vial with its child-resistant cap and asks the technician to put her refill in it to cut down on waste. Under federal poison prevention packaging rules, what should the technician do?

  1. Reuse the vial and fit a new child-resistant cap
  2. Reuse the vial after the pharmacist inspects it
  3. Fill it into a new vial with a new cap
  4. Reuse the vial and cap if both look intact

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because a worn cap is the usual worry, but the vial is part of the special packaging, and a used plastic vial with a new cap is still reused packaging.
  • B. Tempting because pharmacist review often settles a question, but no inspection makes reuse of special packaging lawful, so this adds a step without fixing the problem.
  • C. Correct. The federal rule says special packaging shall not be reused, so the refill goes into a new child-resistant vial and closure.
  • D. Tempting because the cap may seem to work, but the rule does not turn on appearance; reuse is not allowed, and wear is not visible from outside.

Child-resistant closures are tested to keep working through the number of openings and closings expected in normal use, and the threads and locking lugs of a used vial and cap wear down. For that reason 16 CFR 1700.15(c) says special packaging subject to the rule shall not be reused. The technician can explain the reason, offer recycling if the pharmacy has a program, and package the refill in a new vial and closure. A patient who has trouble with the cap may still ask for a non-child-resistant closure, which is a separate, lawful request.

Sources: 16 CFR Part 1700 (poison prevention packaging)

Checking a DEA number

  1. Two letters, then seven digits. First letter: A, B or F for most prescribers, M for mid-level practitioners (NP, PA).
  2. Second letter: almost always the first letter of the prescriber's last name.
  3. Add digits 1, 3 and 5. Add digits 2, 4 and 6 and double that sum. Add the two results.
  4. The last digit of the total must match the 7th digit (the check digit).

Made-up example, Dr. Kim, FK2846193: 2 + 4 + 1 = 7; (8 + 6 + 9) × 2 = 46; 7 + 46 = 53. The last digit is 3, the 7th digit is 3, and K matches Kim, so the number passes. FK2846195 would fail.

Records, storage and disposal

  • Keep every controlled-substance record at least 2 years (states may require longer).
  • C-II records and paper prescriptions are filed separately. C-III to C-V: separate file, or readily retrievable (red 'C' at least 1 inch high, waived if the computer can retrieve them).
  • Biennial inventory at least every 2 years: exact count for C-II; C-III to C-V may be estimated unless an open container holds over 1,000 units.
  • Store in a locked, substantial cabinet, or disperse through the noncontrolled stock.
  • Outdated stock goes to a reverse distributor (it issues a 222 for C-II) or is destroyed on site with Form 41. Patients' drugs go only into an authorized collector's receptacle or a mail-back package.
Safety Federal law gives these steps to the pharmacist: judging whether a controlled prescription is legitimate, taking emergency C-II calls, transferring prescriptions, and selling exempt C-V products without a prescription. Bring a forged-looking prescription or a failed DEA number check to the pharmacist, who decides what happens next. Your state decides what else a technician may do.

The Controlled Substances Act and the DEA

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA, 21 U.S.C. 801 and following) sets up a 'closed system': every manufacturer, distributor, pharmacy and prescriber that handles controlled substances must register with the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), unless exempt, and account for every transaction. The DEA writes the regulations in 21 CFR Part 1300 onward and lists every controlled substance by schedule in 21 CFR 1308.11 to 1308.15. A drug's schedule depends on whether it has an accepted medical use in the US, its abuse potential and how much dependence it causes.

Federal and state law run side by side. Following federal law does not excuse breaking a stricter state law (21 CFR 1307.02), so a pharmacy follows whichever rule is stricter. States may schedule drugs the federal list leaves out, keep a drug in a higher schedule after DEA moves it, shorten the time a prescription stays valid, or limit quantities. Every pharmacy location registers separately, and the registration certificate is kept at that location.

Schedule V also covers small amounts of narcotics sold without a prescription where state law allows it ('exempt narcotics'). Only a pharmacist may make the sale; a technician or cashier may ring it up afterward. The buyer must be at least 18 and show identification if not known to the pharmacist. Within any 48 hours one buyer may get no more than 240 mL (8 oz) or 48 dosage units of an opium-containing product, or 120 mL (4 oz) or 24 dosage units of any other. Each sale goes in a bound record book: buyer's name and address, product and quantity, date, and the pharmacist's name or initials. Where any federal, state or local law requires a prescription for the product, this sale is not allowed.

What a valid controlled-substance prescription needs

  • Dated and signed on the day it is issued.
  • Patient's full name and address.
  • Prescriber's full name, address and DEA registration number.
  • Drug name, strength, dosage form, quantity prescribed and directions for use.
  • Number of refills authorized, if any.

A paper prescription is written in ink or indelible pencil, typed or printed, and signed by hand; a computer-printed or faxed prescription still needs a handwritten signature. A nurse or secretary may prepare a prescription for the prescriber to sign, but only the prescriber decides that it is needed. Residents and staff who prescribe under a hospital's DEA registration add the hospital's number plus the internal code the hospital assigned them. Military and Public Health Service prescribers working in their official duties write their branch and service ID number instead of a DEA number.

The prescriber must issue the prescription for a legitimate medical purpose in the usual course of practice. The pharmacist has a 'corresponding responsibility' for the same thing and may refuse a prescription of doubtful legitimacy. Warning signs DEA lists include prescriptions that look 'too good' or photocopied, mixed inks or handwriting, unusual quantities or directions, patients coming back too often, and several people arriving with similar prescriptions from one prescriber. A correct DEA check digit does not prove a prescription is real: a forger can calculate one. The pharmacy can confirm a registration through DEA's online registration validation tool, and states run prescription drug monitoring programs (PDMPs) that show a patient's controlled-substance fills across pharmacies.

Labels on dispensed controlled substances show the date filled, pharmacy name and address, prescription number, patient's name, prescriber's name, directions and any cautions. C-II, C-III and C-IV containers also carry the federal warning: 'Caution: Federal law prohibits the transfer of this drug to any person other than the patient for whom it was prescribed.' A controlled prescription may be handed only to the patient or a member of the patient's household.

Schedule II in detail

A C-II drug needs a prescription signed by hand or an electronic prescription that meets DEA's EPCS rules. A fax may start the work, but the original signed prescription must be in the pharmacy and checked against the fax before the drug is dispensed. Three exceptions let the fax serve as the original: a C-II narcotic to be compounded for infusion (IV, IM, subcut or intraspinal), a C-II for a resident of a long-term care facility (LTCF), and a C-II narcotic for a hospice patient (the prescription says 'hospice patient').

C-II prescriptions cannot be refilled. Instead, a prescriber may write several prescriptions at once for up to a 90-day supply in total, each marked with the earliest date it may be filled, if state law allows. Federal law sets no expiration date for a C-II prescription, but the pharmacist must judge that the patient still needs it; many states set a limit.

In every case the total of all partial fills cannot exceed the quantity written. DEA's position on the 72-hour rule is that the balance must be ready within 72 hours, though the patient does not have to pick it up in that time. A requested partial fill is allowed only if state law does not forbid it.

An emergency means the prescriber has decided that immediate treatment is needed, no other drug (including a non-C-II drug) will do, and a written prescription is not possible right now. The pharmacist writes the oral order down at once with everything but the signature, and if the prescriber is unknown, makes a reasonable effort to confirm the caller, for example by calling back at a listed number. The quantity covers only the emergency period. The follow-up prescription must arrive within 7 days (if mailed, postmarked within 7 days), carry 'Authorization for Emergency Dispensing' and the date of the oral order, and is attached to the written-down oral order. If it never comes, the pharmacist must tell DEA.

Schedules III to V in detail

C-III to C-V prescriptions may come as signed paper, a fax of a signed prescription, an electronic prescription, or a phone call from the prescriber or the prescriber's agent that the pharmacist promptly writes down. C-III and C-IV prescriptions may be refilled up to 5 times within 6 months of the date written, whichever comes first; after that a new prescription is needed. The prescriber may phone in more refills as long as the total stays within 5 refills and 6 months. Each refill is recorded with the date, the quantity and the dispensing pharmacist's initials; initials and a date alone mean the full amount was dispensed.

For C-V, federal law sets no refill limit or expiration: refills are as the prescriber authorizes. Many states apply the C-III and C-IV limits to C-V as well. Partial fills of C-III to C-V are allowed if each is recorded like a refill, the total does not exceed the amount written, and nothing is dispensed more than 6 months after the date written.

Refill transfers of C-III to C-V prescriptions are allowed once only, communicated directly between two licensed pharmacists. Pharmacies that share a real-time online database (for example, stores of one chain) may transfer as many times as refills remain. The sending pharmacist voids the prescription and records where it went, to whom, the date and their own name; the receiving pharmacist writes 'transfer' and records the original date, refills authorized and remaining, previous fills and both pharmacies' DEA numbers and prescription numbers. Records are kept 2 years from the last refill. Paper, fax and oral prescriptions cannot be transferred before the first fill.

Since 2023, an electronic prescription for C-II to C-V that has never been filled may be transferred once between retail pharmacies at the patient's request, in its electronic form only and without changing its contents. Any refills on a C-III to C-V prescription go with it. Both pharmacies keep the transfer records for 2 years, and the transfer must be allowed by state law.

Electronic prescriptions for controlled substances (EPCS)

  • The prescriber signs in with two of three factors: something they know (a password), something they are (a fingerprint or iris scan), something they have (a hard token separate from the computer). The prescriber may not share the token or password with anyone.
  • Both the prescriber's software and the pharmacy's software must pass a third-party audit or certification against DEA's rules before they are used for controlled substances.
  • If any required information is changed in transmission, the prescription is invalid.
  • The pharmacy decides which employees may enter or annotate controlled-substance dispensing records and sets the software's access controls to match.
  • Notes a pharmacist would write on paper (partial fills, transfers, emergency authorization) are made in the electronic record, and the records stay electronic for 2 years.
  • If a paper or phoned prescription says it was first sent electronically, the pharmacy checks that the electronic one was not also filled, and voids one of them.

Ordering, receiving and moving stock

C-I and C-II stock is ordered on a DEA Form 222 or through CSOS. The form is pre-printed with the pharmacy's name, address and DEA number. Each numbered line holds one item; the number of lines used goes at the bottom; one supplier per form. It is signed by the registrant (for a company, a partner or officer) or by someone given power of attorney. The power of attorney is signed by the registrant (or that partner or officer), the person receiving the power and two witnesses, and is kept with the 222 records. The pharmacy keeps a copy and sends the original to the supplier. When the drugs arrive, the number of containers received and the date are recorded on the pharmacy's copy. A form with any error, erasure or change cannot be corrected; a new one is used. Partial shipments must be completed within 60 days; no 222 is valid more than 60 days after the purchaser executes it. Lost or stolen forms are reported to DEA at once with their serial numbers.

CSOS orders are signed with a DEA-issued digital certificate held by an authorized person, and the records are kept electronically for 2 years. DEA's CSOS page says it can carry orders for C-I to C-V. C-III to C-V stock ordered without CSOS needs only the invoice or packing slip, marked with the date received and checked for accuracy, and kept readily retrievable.

One pharmacy may send controlled stock to another registrant without a distributor registration if the total it distributes in a calendar year stays at or under 5% of all the controlled dosage units it dispenses (the 'five percent rule'). The receiving registrant sends a 222 for C-II; for C-III to C-V both sides keep a record with the names, addresses and DEA numbers of both. When a pharmacy closes or is sold, a complete inventory taken on the day of transfer is the closing inventory for one and the opening inventory for the other.

Inventory and records

  • Initial inventory on the day the pharmacy first handles controlled substances (a zero inventory if none); biennial inventory on any date within 2 years of the last one; a separate inventory on the date a newly scheduled drug becomes controlled.
  • Each inventory notes whether it was taken at the opening or close of business, and lists each drug, its strength and form, the units per container and the number of containers. It is kept at the pharmacy, not sent to DEA.
  • Records kept at least 2 years: executed 222s and CSOS orders, powers of attorney, C-III to C-V invoices, inventories, records of drugs sent to other registrants or reverse distributors, prescriptions and the exempt C-V record book, Forms 106 and 41, the registration certificate, and the pseudoephedrine logbook.
  • Federal law does not require a perpetual (running) inventory; state law or company policy often does, especially for C-II.

Security, theft and loss

Pharmacies store C-II to C-V drugs in a securely locked, substantially built cabinet or spread them through the noncontrolled stock so that theft is harder. A pharmacy may not let anyone handle controlled substances who has a felony conviction involving controlled substances or who had a DEA registration denied, revoked or surrendered for cause, unless DEA grants a waiver.

For a theft or significant loss, the pharmacy notifies its DEA field office directly in writing within one business day of discovery, then files Form 106 online within 45 days. DEA also advises telling local police and the state board. 'Significant' is not defined: the pharmacy weighs the amount, the drug, whether the loss points to particular people, any pattern of small losses over time and the local diversion risk. Small repeated losses can add up to a significant one. If the investigation finds there was no loss, no Form 106 is needed, but DEA suggests telling the field office in writing. Clerical miscounts are logged in the pharmacy, not reported on Form 106. A shipment lost in transit is the supplier's to report until the pharmacy signs for it; after that it is the pharmacy's.

Witnessed breakage or spillage is not a loss because the drug is accounted for. If the drug can be recovered it is destroyed or sent to a reverse distributor; if it cannot, DEA expects the event recorded on Form 41 with two witnesses.

Disposal and take-back

A pharmacy disposes of its own controlled stock in one of four ways: destroy it on site so it is non-retrievable, with two employees handling and witnessing it and a Form 41 record; send it to a DEA-registered reverse distributor; return it to the supplier or manufacturer for a return or recall; or ask DEA's local Special Agent in Charge for help. For C-II sent to a reverse distributor, the reverse distributor issues the 222 to the pharmacy; for C-III to C-V the pharmacy keeps a record of what was sent. The reverse distributor files its own Form 41 when it destroys the drugs; the pharmacy does not use a Form 41 for the transfer.

A pharmacy cannot take back a patient's controlled drugs over the counter. To collect them it must modify its DEA registration to become an authorized collector (no fee) and install a collection receptacle inside the pharmacy near the area where controlled drugs are kept and staff are present, or at a long-term care facility. Receptacles are bolted down, locked, accept only C-II to C-V (and noncontrolled drugs if the pharmacy chooses), and are locked when no employee is present. Two employees install and remove the inner liners (at a long-term care facility, one pharmacy employee and one facility supervisor may), which are sealed at once, never opened or sorted, and recorded by their ID number. A pharmacy can instead hand out mail-back packages from a reverse distributor's program, which needs no registration change. A long-term care facility puts a resident's discontinued controlled drugs into its receptacle within 3 business days.
