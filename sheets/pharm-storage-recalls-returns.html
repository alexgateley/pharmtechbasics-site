Storage temperatures (USP terms)

'Excursions permitted' covers brief swings, as in shipping, not a warmer place to keep stock.
Label termCelsiusFahrenheit
Freezer-25 to -10 °C-13 to 14 °F
Refrigerator (a cold place)2 to 8 °C36 to 46 °F
Cool8 to 15 °C46 to 59 °F
Controlled room temperature20 to 25 °C; excursions 15 to 30 °C allowed68 to 77 °F; excursions 59 to 86 °F
Warm30 to 40 °C86 to 104 °F
Excessive heatAbove 40 °CAbove 104 °F
  • Light: keep the product in its carton or a light-resistant container until use. Latanoprost, insulin and many vaccines say 'protect from light'.
  • Moisture and potency: dispense in a tight container when the label says so. Nitroglycerin sublingual tablets stay in the original glass bottle, capped tightly after each use, to keep their potency. Dabigatran capsules stay in the original bottle or blister to protect them from moisture, never a pill organizer.
  • Restricted access: C-II to C-V go in a locked, sturdy cabinet, or a pharmacy may disperse them through the non-controlled stock (21 CFR 1301.75).
Exam tip Know two pairs cold: refrigerator 2 to 8 °C = 36 to 46 °F, and controlled room temperature 20 to 25 °C = 68 to 77 °F.

Vaccines and temperature monitoring (CDC)

  • Ranges: refrigerator 2 to 8 °C (36 to 46 °F); vaccine freezer -50 to -15 °C (-58 to 5 °F); ultra-cold -90 to -60 °C. Check each package insert.
  • Monitor with a digital data logger (DDL): buffered probe in the center of the vaccines, display outside the door, alarm, min/max display.
  • At the start of each workday, check and record the min and max temperature, date, time and your name. Keep logs 3 years.
  • Never use a dormitory-style or bar-style refrigerator-freezer. No food or drinks in the unit. Earliest expiration dates in front.
Safety On an excursion: tell the vaccine coordinator or supervisor at once, label the vaccines 'DO NOT USE', keep them at the correct temperature, apart from other stock, and do not discard them. Only the manufacturer or the immunization program can say they are still usable.

Try one

Storage, Recalls, Returns and Disposal practice question

A community pharmacy technician receives an e-prescription for Suboxone 4 mg/1 mg sublingual film, one film daily. Under federal law, which generic ingredients and schedule match this prescription?

  1. Buprenorphine as a single ingredient, Schedule III
  2. Buprenorphine and naloxone, Schedule III
  3. Naltrexone, not a federally controlled substance
  4. Buprenorphine and naloxone, Schedule II

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The schedule is right, but the 4 mg/1 mg strength shows a second ingredient; a buprenorphine-only sublingual tablet is labeled with one number, such as 8 mg.
  • B. Correct. Suboxone combines buprenorphine and naloxone, and buprenorphine products are Schedule III under 21 CFR 1308.13.
  • C. Naltrexone (Vivitrol injection) also treats opioid use disorder and is not controlled, but it is an antagonist, not the drug in Suboxone.
  • D. The ingredients are right, and many opioids such as methadone and oxycodone are Schedule II, but buprenorphine is listed in Schedule III.

Suboxone sublingual film contains buprenorphine, a partial opioid agonist, and naloxone, an opioid antagonist added to discourage injection. The label lists treatment of opioid dependence with counseling and psychosocial support, in strengths of 2/0.5, 4/1, 8/2 and 12/3 mg, placed whole under the tongue or inside the cheek. Under federal law buprenorphine is a Schedule III narcotic (21 CFR 1308.13, DEA code 9064), so the prescription follows Schedule III rules for refills and records. In each Suboxone strength the first number is buprenorphine and the second naloxone, which helps when entering the product.

Sources: SUBOXONE (buprenorphine and naloxone) Sublingual Film Prescribing Information (Indivior, rev 12/2025); 21 CFR Part 1308 (schedules of controlled substances)

Expiration and in-use dates

A date given as month and year means the last day of that month: 12/2026 is good through December 31, 2026. An in-use or beyond-use date replaces the expiration date but never extends it: use whichever comes first.

Product (label)Before opening or mixingAfter opening or mixing
Insulin lispro pen (Humalog KwikPen)Refrigerate: good to the expiration date. Room temperature (up to 30 °C): 28 days28 days at room temperature, counting any unopened days out of the refrigerator; do not refrigerate
Amoxicillin oral suspensionDry powder at controlled room temperatureDiscard after 14 days; refrigeration preferable, not required
Amoxicillin-clavulanate suspensionDry powder at or below 25 °CRefrigerate; discard after 10 days
Latanoprost eye drops (Xalatan)Refrigerate 2 to 8 °C, protected from lightRoom temperature up to 25 °C for 6 weeks
Dabigatran capsule bottle (Pradaxa)Controlled room temperature, original bottleUse within 4 months of opening

Rotate stock first-expired, first-out: shortest date to the front, newest delivery behind it. Pull expired product at once and set it apart for return or disposal.

Recalls and market withdrawals (21 CFR 7.3)

TypeHealth riskUsual depth
Class IReasonable probability of serious harm or deathConsumer (patient) level
Class IITemporary or medically reversible harm; serious harm remoteRetail (pharmacy) level
Class IIINot likely to cause harm (for example a labeling error)Wholesale level
Market withdrawalMinor or no violation; FDA would not take legal actionSet by the firm
  1. Match the notice to your stock by NDC and lot number (shelves, will-call, robots, the refrigerator).
  2. Pull every matching unit, label it 'Recalled: do not dispense' and quarantine it.
  3. Follow the firm's instructions (return or destroy), send back its response form even if you had none, and keep a record.
  4. For a patient-level recall, the pharmacist decides who is contacted and answers questions about stopping the drug.
Exam tip Class I is the most dangerous. Class numbers rank the hazard; the recalling firm, not the pharmacy, decides what happens to the product.

Returns

ProductWhere it goes
Saleable overstock or product ordered in errorCredit return to the wholesaler it came from, under the wholesaler's policy
Expired, damaged or recalled stockNonsaleable return: manufacturer, wholesaler, or a reverse distributor (returns processor) that arranges credit and destruction
Prescription filled but never picked upReverse the claim; return to stock only as state law and pharmacy policy allow, with the drug, lot and expiration still identifiable
Controlled substance from a patientNever back to stock: only an authorized collection receptacle or mail-back package, then destruction

Non-returnable items are set by the wholesaler's or manufacturer's policy, not federal law. For vaccines, open or broken vials and syringes, activated manufacturer-filled syringes and predrawn doses cannot be returned (CDC). C-II returns need a DEA Form 222 (or CSOS order) from the receiver.

Disposal (federal rules)

WasteWhat happens
Pharmacy's own controlled stockReverse distributor, return to supplier, or on-site destruction to non-retrievable with two employees witnessing, recorded on DEA Form 41
Patients' unwanted drugsAuthorized collector's receptacle, mail-back envelope or Take Back Day; if none, FDA flush list drugs are flushed, others mixed with litter or coffee grounds in a sealed bag in the trash
Hazardous waste pharmaceuticals (P- and U-listed, characteristic)Never down the drain (EPA sewer ban since August 21, 2019); hazardous waste container, or a reverse distributor if potentially creditable
SharpsClosable, puncture-resistant, leakproof, labeled or color-coded container, upright and not overfilled (OSHA)
Safety Never take a patient's controlled drugs back over the counter, and never pour hazardous drugs down a sink. States may be stricter than these federal rules.

Documentation

  • Temperature logs with excursions and actions (CDC: 3 years).
  • Recall notices, what was pulled and returned, and the response sent.
  • Controlled substance returns, Form 222s and Form 41s (DEA: at least 2 years).
  • Collection receptacle inner liner records, signed by the two employees.
  • DSCSA transaction information and statements (6 years).

Reading a storage statement

Many solid oral drug labels read 'Store at 20° to 25°C (68° to 77°F); excursions permitted to 15° to 30°C (59° to 86°F) [see USP Controlled Room Temperature]'. The USP definition behind it keeps the mean kinetic temperature (MKT), a weighted average that counts warm periods more heavily, at or below 25 °C. Excursions are for short swings such as shipping or a warm afternoon, not a reason to store stock at 28 °C. Authors quoting the USP General Notices describe brief spikes up to 40 °C as acceptable only when they are transient (under 24 hours) and the manufacturer allows them.

Label wordingWhat the technician does
Store at controlled room temperatureShelf stock, 20 to 25 °C; check the room thermometer and log if your pharmacy keeps one
Store in a refrigerator, 2 to 8 °C. Do not freezeRefrigerator, not directly under the cooling vent; in a household-grade unit, keep vaccines off the door shelves and out of the drawers (CDC)
Dispense in a tight, light-resistant container as defined in the USPAmber vial with a tight closure; child-resistant closure as required
Keep in the original containerDispense the manufacturer's bottle or blister, not a vial (nitroglycerin, dabigatran)
Protect from lightKeep it in its carton or other light-resistant packaging until it is used

Shipping allowances belong to the label too. Unopened latanoprost is stored in the refrigerator, but during shipment to the patient the bottle may sit at up to 40 °C (104 °F) for no more than 8 days. Once opened, the bottle keeps 6 weeks at room temperature up to 25 °C (77 °F).

Exam tip Exams like the gap between cool (8 to 15 °C) and controlled room temperature (20 to 25 °C), and like asking where a product goes when the label says 'Do not freeze'.

Some vaccines run on a beyond-use date (BUD) after they change state. Count the transition day as day 0, write the BUD on the label with your initials, and use the earlier of the BUD and the expiration date. CDC's examples: varicella-containing vaccine is given within 30 minutes of reconstitution and MMR (not MMRV) within 8 hours. A manufacturer can also shorten an expiration date after an excursion or extend it with new stability data; the new date is written on the vial, initialed and entered in the inventory.

  • Excursion record: date and time, unit and room temperatures with min/max, who reported it, what happened, how long (from the DDL), the affected vaccines with lot numbers and whether publicly funded, other items in the unit, and any earlier problems.
  • Then: fix the unit per the SOP, check the probe is centered, contact the immunization program or manufacturer with the log data, and record what you were told and where each dose ended up (used with a new date, discarded or returned).
  • Expired or compromised vaccine may sometimes be returned for credit even if it must be discarded; ask the immunization program or the manufacturer.

Expiration dates and in-use dating

Federal manufacturing rules require every drug product to carry an expiration date set by stability testing and tied to the storage conditions on its label (21 CFR 211.137). The date goes on the immediate container and on the outer package unless it can be read through it (21 CFR 201.17). A product reconstituted at dispensing must carry expiration information for both the dry and the mixed forms, which is why suspension labels give a discard time after mixing.

Label detailWhy it matters
Humalog: room-temperature time counts toward 28 days whether the pen is opened or notA pen left out unopened for 10 days has 18 days left after it is opened
Humalog and Lantus pens in use: room temperature onlyPatients keep the pen they are using out of the refrigerator; spares stay refrigerated
Humalog and Lantus vials in use: refrigerated or room temperature, 28 daysThe 28-day limit applies either way
Tresiba (insulin degludec) pens and vials: 56 days unopened at room temperature, and 56 days in use, refrigerated or at room temperatureIn-use times differ between insulins; read each label rather than assuming 28 days
Amoxicillin: 14 days, refrigeration optionalRefrigeration is preferable but not required; the 14 days apply either way
Amoxicillin-clavulanate: 10 days, refrigeratedThe shorter time and the refrigeration are both required
Dabigatran: 4 months after the bottle is openedMoisture degrades it; it is not repackaged into vials or organizers

When you reconstitute a product, put the discard date, counted from the day it is mixed, on the label as your pharmacy's procedure directs. Recalls and expiration checks work by lot number and date, so keep both traceable for anything taken out of its original package.

How a recall reaches the pharmacy

A recall is a firm's removal or correction of a marketed product that FDA considers to violate the law it enforces. It may be started by the firm, requested by FDA, or ordered by FDA where a statute allows. FDA assigns the class and oversees the firm's recall strategy, which sets the depth (wholesale, retail, or consumer level), whether a public warning is issued, and the effectiveness checks that confirm each customer was reached. Two related terms: a market withdrawal is a removal for a minor violation or none (FDA's example is tampering with no sign of a manufacturing problem), and a stock recovery is a removal of product that never left the firm's control.

  • The notice comes from the recalling firm or the wholesaler, marked 'Drug Recall' and 'Urgent' for Class I and II. It names the product, size, lot numbers and the reason, and says what to do.
  • Federal rules expect a consignee (anyone who received the product) to carry out the instructions at once and, where needed, pass the recall on to its own customers.
  • All recalls appear in FDA's Enforcement Report; widely distributed or serious ones are also announced on FDA.gov.
  • For Class II and III recalls, FDA says patients can usually keep taking the medicine unless told otherwise; stopping some drugs is more harmful than continuing. That conversation belongs to the pharmacist and the prescriber.

Worked example: a Class II retail-level recall lists one lot of a blood pressure tablet. The technician searches the shelf, will-call and any automated dispensing cabinet for that NDC and lot, finds one stock bottle and one filled prescription not yet picked up, pulls both, labels them and puts them in the quarantine area, completes the firm's response form, and tells the pharmacist about the will-call prescription so it can be refilled from a different lot. Prescriptions already picked up are handled as the notice and the pharmacist direct.

Safety A product you suspect is counterfeit, diverted or stolen is a DSCSA suspect product: quarantine it apart from saleable stock and tell the pharmacist, who investigates with the trading partner and notifies FDA if it proves illegitimate.

Returns, credit and reverse distributors

Under the DSCSA a dispenser may return saleable product to the trading partner it bought from, and may return nonsaleable product to the manufacturer or repackager, the wholesaler, or a returns processor, without passing tracing paperwork the other way. Before reselling a saleable return, the wholesaler must verify its product identifier (the serialized code on each package or sealed case), so send returns in their original labeled packages.

TermMeaning
Credit returnProduct sent back to the wholesaler or manufacturer for a credit on the account, under its return policy
Return to stockA never-dispensed prescription put back into inventory after the claim is reversed; state law and policy decide how and for how long
Reverse distributor (DEA)A DEA registrant that takes controlled substances from another registrant to return them to the manufacturer or destroy them
Reverse distributor (EPA)A business that receives potentially creditable hazardous waste prescription drugs to arrange or verify manufacturer credit
Potentially creditable (EPA)A prescription hazardous waste drug with a reasonable expectation of manufacturer credit: in original manufacturer packaging (recalled drugs excepted), never dispensed, and unexpired or less than one year past expiration
  • Controlled substances to a reverse distributor: for C-II the reverse distributor issues a DEA Form 222 (or electronic CSOS order) to the pharmacy; for C-III to C-V the pharmacy keeps a record of the drug name, dosage form, strength, quantity and date. Two employees load or watch the loading until the transfer is complete.
  • The pharmacy does not use Form 41 for that transfer; the registrant that destroys the drug records the destruction on Form 41.
  • Check before you pack: the wholesaler or manufacturer policy decides what earns credit (opened or partial packages, cold-chain items, very short-dated or long-expired stock, and controlled substances are the usual questions).

Controlled substance disposal and take-back

SituationFederal rule

Form 41 retentionKept with the pharmacy's records at least 2 years; DEA's form page says it is not submitted unless DEA asks

Witnessed breakage, not recoverableRecord it on Form 41 signed by two people who saw it
Theft or significant lossNot a disposal: the pharmacy notifies the DEA Field Division Office in writing within one business day of discovery and files DEA Form 106

  • To collect patients' controlled drugs, a retail pharmacy modifies its DEA registration to become an authorized collector (no fee). Only C-II to C-V lawfully held by the patient may go in; non-controlled drugs may be mixed in.
  • The receptacle is inside the pharmacy, near where controlled substances are kept and an employee is present (for example, visible from the counter), fastened to a permanent structure so it cannot be removed, locked, with a small slot that is locked when the pharmacy is closed.
  • The patient drops the drug in personally. Staff never take it from the patient and never count, sort or inventory what is inside.
  • Two employees install and remove the inner liner, seal it at once and sign the liner records (liner ID number, size, dates). Sealed liners are stored locked until destruction.
  • Pharmacies may also hand out mail-back packages from a reverse distributor's program without becoming a collector. National Prescription Drug Take Back Days are run through DEA with law enforcement collection sites.
Exam tip Form 41 records destruction; Form 222 moves C-II drugs, including to a reverse distributor; Form 106 reports theft or loss. Exams mix these three up.

Hazardous waste pharmaceuticals (EPA Subpart P)

EPA's 2019 rule (40 CFR part 266 subpart P) treats pharmacies, including long-term care and mail-order pharmacies, as healthcare facilities. A pharmacy that is a small or large quantity generator must follow Subpart P once its state adopts it; a very small quantity generator may opt in. States must adopt Subpart P before it applies there and may be stricter. The sewer ban is different: since August 21, 2019, no healthcare facility in any state or territory may put hazardous waste pharmaceuticals down a drain.

Rule pointDetail
P-list (acute hazardous) examplesWarfarin above 0.3%, nicotine and its salts, arsenic trioxide, physostigmine
OTC nicotine replacementPatches, gum and lozenges are no longer hazardous waste (once the state adopts the exemption)
Empty containersStock and dispensing bottles of up to 10,000 pills, vials and ampules up to 1 liter, and unit-dose packages, emptied the usual way, are not hazardous waste; empty warfarin stock bottles go in the trash; triple rinsing of P-listed drug containers is no longer allowed
Expired hazardous drugsBecome waste on the expiration date, or sooner once the decision to discard them is made; if potentially creditable they wait in the returns area for a reverse distributor, otherwise they go straight into a hazardous waste container
Hazardous waste controlled substancesConditionally exempt from EPA hazardous waste rules when they are never sewered, are handled under DEA rules, and are burned at one of the permitted combustor types (40 CFR 266.506)

Chemotherapy and other hazardous drugs also fall under the facility's hazardous drug handling program (USP <800>, on its own page). Handling safety and EPA waste status are separate questions: where such a drug is also RCRA hazardous waste, Subpart P applies to its disposal.

Helping patients with disposal and sharps

  1. Best option: a take-back location (a pharmacy receptacle or police station) or a prepaid mail-back envelope.
  2. No take-back option nearby: check FDA's flush list. It names drugs that are sought for misuse and can kill with one dose, such as fentanyl patches, buprenorphine, oxycodone, morphine and methadone products. Only those are flushed.
  3. Everything else: mix with an unappealing substance such as dirt, cat litter or used coffee grounds (do not crush tablets), seal in a plastic bag, put it in the trash, and scratch personal information off the empty bottle.

Household sharps go straight into a sharps disposal container, which is kept out of reach of children and pets and disposed of by community rules when about three-quarters full; it is not reused. In the pharmacy (for example at an immunization station), OSHA requires used needles to go at once into a closable, puncture-resistant, leakproof, labeled container kept upright near where sharps are used, replaced before it overfills, and closed before it is moved. Contaminated needles are not bent or recapped; OSHA allows recapping only by a one-handed technique or a device when no alternative is feasible.

Safety A technician can point patients to take-back options and FDA's guidance. A question about whether a specific drug is safe to flush, crush or keep belongs to the pharmacist.

Exam pearls

  • Refrigerator 2 to 8 °C (36 to 46 °F); freezer -25 to -10 °C; cool 8 to 15 °C; controlled room temperature 20 to 25 °C (68 to 77 °F); excessive heat above 40 °C (104 °F).
  • Exp 05/2027 means use through May 31, 2027.
  • Class I recall = serious harm or death; Class III = unlikely to cause harm; market withdrawal = minor or no violation.
  • Recalled product is pulled and quarantined by NDC and lot; the firm's notice says return or destroy.
  • Vaccine excursion: label 'do not use', keep cold, do not discard, call the manufacturer or immunization program.
  • Digital data logger, buffered probe, min/max at the start of every workday, logs kept 3 years.
  • Reconstituted amoxicillin 14 days; amoxicillin-clavulanate 10 days in the refrigerator; Humalog and Lantus pens 28 days at room temperature, but Tresiba 56 days: insulin in-use times differ, so read the label.
  • Pharmacy's own controlled stock: reverse distributor or on-site destruction with Form 41 and two witnesses. Patients' controlled drugs: collection receptacle or mail-back only.
  • No hazardous waste pharmaceuticals down the drain, ever (EPA, since August 21, 2019).
