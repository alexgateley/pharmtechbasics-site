In depth

Setting up any calculation

  1. Write down what you have and what you want, each with its unit.
  2. Convert everything to the same units first: pounds to kilograms, teaspoons to mL, grams to mg.
  3. Set up a proportion, or multiply by conversion fractions so the unwanted units cancel (dimensional analysis).
  4. Estimate before you trust the number: a child's dose should be smaller than an adult's, and a days supply of 300 for a 30-tablet bottle is wrong.
  5. Round only at the end, using the rule the question or the pharmacy gives.

Dimensional analysis example: a patient needs 400 mg from a 250 mg/5 mL suspension. 400 mg × (5 mL ÷ 250 mg) = 8 mL. The mg cancel and mL are left, which is the unit you want.

Roman numerals still appear in quantities on prescriptions (PTCE 4.1).

Days supply and quantity

  • Round down to whole days: 100 tablets at 3 a day last 33 full days, and the supply runs out on day 34.
  • As-needed sigs: use the most the sig allows in a day. Ranges ('1 to 2 tablets'): use the higher number.
  • Tapers: add the steps. Prednisone 10 mg tablets, 40 mg daily for 3 days, then 30, 20 and 10 mg daily for 3 days each: (4 + 3 + 2 + 1) × 3 = 30 tablets over 12 days.
  • Multiple daily doses of one drug: potassium chloride ER 20 mEq tablets, 40 mEq a day given as 20 mEq twice daily (the label limits a single dose to 20 mEq): 2 tablets a day, 60 for 30 days.
  • Payers set their own days-supply rules, and a wrong days supply can cause an early-refill rejection or fail an audit. When a sig is unclear, the pharmacist clarifies it with the prescriber.

Weight-based and pediatric doses

  1. Convert pounds to kilograms (÷ 2.2).
  2. Multiply by the mg/kg dose. Check whether it is per day or per dose.
  3. Divide a daily dose by the number of doses per day.
  4. Convert the dose to a volume with the concentration.
  5. Multiply by doses per day and days for the quantity to dispense, and choose the package that covers it.

Worked example from the amoxicillin label's severe-infection regimen (45 mg/kg/day divided every 12 hours): a 16 kg child gets 16 × 45 = 720 mg a day, 360 mg a dose. With 400 mg/5 mL (80 mg/mL), 360 ÷ 80 = 4.5 mL twice daily. For 10 days: 4.5 × 2 × 10 = 90 mL, so dispense the 100 mL bottle. The same label gives 25 mg/kg/day every 12 hours or 20 mg/kg/day every 8 hours for milder infections; the pharmacist checks which regimen and dose apply.

RuleFormulaUsed forExample (adult dose 500 mg)
Fried'sage in months ÷ 150 × adult doseInfants under 1 year9 months: 9 ÷ 150 × 500 = 30 mg
Young'sage in years ÷ (age + 12) × adult doseChildren 1 to 12 years6 years: 6 ÷ 18 × 500 = 166.7 mg

Safety These rules are rough estimates from an adult dose. A 2023 modeling study found the age-based rules (Fried's, Young's) would underdose valproate in neonates, infants and adolescents, and the BSA rule could overdose the youngest children. Real pediatric doses come from the label's mg/kg or mg/m² dosing, and the pharmacist verifies them.

Body surface area by Mosteller: BSA (m²) = √(height in cm × weight in kg ÷ 3,600). For 170 cm and 70 kg: 170 × 70 = 11,900; ÷ 3,600 = 3.306; √3.306 = 1.82 m². Chemotherapy and some other drugs are dosed in mg/m²: at 100 mg/m², 1.82 × 100 = 182 mg.

Reconstitution and powder volume

A dry powder takes up space once it dissolves, so the final volume is larger than the water added. Powder volume = final volume − diluent volume. The label gives the diluent amount and the concentration after mixing; follow it exactly.

Water volumes from one manufacturer's label (Sandoz); other manufacturers' volumes can differ. The label says to tap the bottle to loosen the powder, add about one third of the water and shake, then add the rest and shake.

Injectable example (made-up numbers): a 1 g vial makes 10 mL of 100 mg/mL when 9.3 mL of diluent is added, so its powder volume is 10 − 9.3 = 0.7 mL. To make 250 mg/mL instead, the final volume must be 1,000 mg ÷ 250 mg/mL = 4 mL, so add 4 − 0.7 = 3.3 mL.

Exam tip Reconstituted products have a short life after mixing: the amoxicillin label says to discard unused suspension after 14 days (refrigeration preferred, not required). Label the bottle with the discard date.

  • Percent w/v to mg/mL: multiply the percent by 10. 0.9% sodium chloride = 9 mg/mL = 9 g per liter.
  • Ratio to mg/mL: 1:1,000 = 1 mg/mL; 1:10,000 = 0.1 mg/mL. Ratio to percent: 1 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 0.1%.
  • Read 'in' versus 'to'. A ratio strength of 1:1,000 is 1 part in 1,000 parts total. A mixing ratio of '1:3, 1 part drug to 3 parts base' (ExCPT wording) is 4 parts in all: 120 g made 1:3 is 30 g of drug and 90 g of base.

Dilution with C1 × V1 = C2 × V2: both strengths must be in the same unit, and the final strength must be weaker than the stock. Example: 500 mL of 20% dextrose from 70%: V1 = 20 × 500 ÷ 70 = 142.86 mL of 70% plus 357.14 mL of diluent.

Alligation (ExCPT only since the January 2026 PTCE outline) mixes a stronger and a weaker product for a strength in between. Subtract across: parts of the stronger = desired − weaker; parts of the weaker = stronger − desired. A diluent or plain base counts as 0%.

IV rates and infusion times

The drop factor is printed on the tubing package. The international standard for gravity sets (ISO 8536-4) is 20 drops of water = 1 mL.
SetDrop factor125 mL/h equals
Macrodrip10 gtt/mL125 × 10 ÷ 60 = 20.8, so 21 gtt/min
Macrodrip15 gtt/mL125 × 15 ÷ 60 = 31.25, so 31 gtt/min
Macrodrip20 gtt/mL125 × 20 ÷ 60 = 41.7, so 42 gtt/min
Microdrip60 gtt/mL125 gtt/min (with 60 gtt/mL, gtt/min = mL/h)
  • Pumps run in mL/h; gravity sets are counted in drops per minute.
  • Dose rate to mL/h: mL/h = dose per hour ÷ concentration. A drug at 100 units/mL ordered at 1,000 units/h runs at 10 mL/h. For a dose per minute, multiply by 60 first: 2 mg/min from 4 mg/mL = 2 × 60 ÷ 4 = 30 mL/h.
  • Volume over a time: 125 mL/h for 24 hours = 3,000 mL, so three 1-liter bags for the day.
  • Infusion time: 1,000 mL at 80 mL/h = 12.5 hours = 12 hours 30 minutes; started at 0800, it ends at 2030.

Milliequivalents

A milliequivalent counts electrical charge: mEq = mg × valence ÷ molecular weight, or mmol × valence. Sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate have a valence of 1, so 1 mmol = 1 mEq; calcium and magnesium have a valence of 2, so 1 mmol = 2 mEq.

SaltMolecular weight, valenceWorked example
Potassium chloride (KCl)74.55, 11,500 mg ÷ 74.55 = 20.1, labeled 20 mEq (KCl ER tablet); 150 mg per 100 mL = 1.5 g/L = 20 mEq/L (KCl in 5% dextrose)
Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3)84.01, 18.4% = 84 mg/mL ÷ 84 = 1 mEq/mL; a 50 mL vial holds 50 mEq
KCl injection concentrate2 mEq/mL on the label40 mEq ÷ 2 mEq/mL = 20 mL, added to a large-volume IV bag
Safety Potassium chloride concentrate is for IV infusion only and must be diluted before use. Its label warns that direct injection at this strength may be instantly fatal, and that fatal arrhythmias and cardiac arrest have followed undiluted doses. It is never given by IV push.

Business math

  • Markup is figured on cost; margin is figured on the selling price. A $24 drug sold at $30 has a $6 markup (25% of cost) and a 20% margin ($6 of $30).
  • Gross profit = selling price − cost of the drug. Net profit = gross profit − the costs of running the pharmacy (staff, rent, supplies, fees).
  • Average wholesale price (AWP) is a list price set by manufacturers and published in pricing compendia; it is not what pharmacies actually pay. Contracts often pay AWP minus a percent plus a dispensing fee. Medicaid fee-for-service programs moved from AWP to actual acquisition cost under a 2016 federal rule, and the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 replaced AWP − 5% with average sales price (ASP) + 6% for Medicare Part B drugs.
  • Inventory turnover = annual purchases at cost ÷ average inventory (many texts use cost of goods sold, which is close to purchases when stock levels stay steady), where average inventory = (beginning + ending inventory) ÷ 2. Days of inventory = 365 ÷ turnover. Higher turnover means less money sitting on the shelf, but too little stock risks running out.
