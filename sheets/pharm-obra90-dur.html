OBRA 90 in brief

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 made each state Medicaid program run a drug use review (DUR) program for outpatient prescriptions. The rules are in 42 CFR 456 Subpart K. The federal text covers Medicaid patients; state law sets the details, and many states apply the counseling rules to every patient.

PartWhenWhat it is
Prospective DURBefore each prescription is filled or handed overScreen the new drug against the patient's profile; offer to counsel; keep a profile
Retrospective DURAt least quarterly, after dispensingThe state reviews claims for patterns of fraud, abuse, overuse or poor care
EducationOngoingThe state DUR Board teaches prescribers and pharmacists about problems it finds

Prospective DUR: the seven screens

Drug-allergy interactionSignificant risk of an allergic reactionAmoxicillin for a patient with a penicillin allergy

Safety The days supply is part of the dose check. Enter it correctly: a wrong days supply makes the software compute a wrong daily dose, so a real overdose can pass, or a correct dose can be flagged.

Try one

OBRA 90: Drug Use Review and the Offer to Counsel practice question

At a community pharmacy, the pharmacist finds that a patient was given a vaccine dose yesterday from a vial that had expired the day before. The patient reports no symptoms. Besides the pharmacy's internal incident report, which reporting route do CDC and FDA encourage for this event?

  1. A VAERS report of the administration error
  2. An error report to the state immunization registry
  3. A MedWatch report on Form FDA 3500
  4. No outside report, since no adverse event occurred

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. VAERS lists vaccine administration errors among the events it encourages health care providers to report, separately from adverse events.
  • B. Tempting because the pharmacy sends dose records to the registry, but a registry records which vaccines a patient received; it is not the national system for errors and reactions.
  • C. Tempting because MedWatch takes reports on most FDA-regulated products, but FDA sends vaccine reports to VAERS instead.
  • D. Tempting because nothing happened to the patient, but VAERS asks providers to report vaccine administration errors as well as adverse events.

The Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System, run by CDC and FDA, accepts reports from anyone. Health care providers are required to report the events in the VAERS Table of Reportable Events and events the manufacturer lists as a contraindication to further doses, and are encouraged to report any adverse event after vaccination and vaccine administration errors. An expired dose is an administration error even without symptoms, so it belongs in VAERS as well as in the internal report. The pharmacist decides on any follow-up for the patient, and the technician gathers the vaccine name, dose, date, time and site of administration, along with the lot number and expiration date from the vial.

Sources: Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS); FDA MedWatch; PTCB PTCE Content Outline (effective January 6, 2026)

The offer to counsel and the profile

  • Offer: made to each patient or caregiver who presents a prescription, in person when practicable or by a toll-free line for long-distance calls (mail order must have one).
  • Counseling: by the pharmacist, on what the pharmacist judges significant: name and description; dosage form, dose, route and duration; directions and precautions; common severe side effects, interactions and contraindications; self-monitoring; storage; refills; what to do after a missed dose.
  • Refusal: the patient may decline; the pharmacist need not counsel. The state decides what is recorded.
  • Profile (a reasonable effort to obtain, record and keep): name, address, phone, date of birth or age, gender; disease states, allergies and drug reactions, all medicines and relevant devices; pharmacist comments.
Exam tip Federal law requires an OFFER to counsel, not counseling. State law decides: new prescriptions only or refills too, whether a technician may make the offer, what is documented, and what happens when the patient is not there (delivery, mail).

In depth

The one-page summary above is what the PDF prints. These sections go further.

Where OBRA 90 came from

OBRA 90 (Public Law 101-508) was a budget law. After a decade of fast drug price inflation in the 1980s, Congress used it to control Medicaid outpatient drug spending. It did three things for prescription drugs: a manufacturer rebate program (started January 1991), a requirement that every state Medicaid program run both prospective and retrospective DUR, and demonstration projects that tested online DUR and paying pharmacists for counseling (Kidder and Bae 1999). Most private insurers already used some form of DUR at the time.

The DUR section is Social Security Act section 1927(g) (42 U.S.C. 1396r-8(g)). The federal Medicaid agency (then HCFA, now CMS) wrote the details into 42 CFR 456 Subpart K (published November 2, 1992), which required each state to have its program running by January 1, 1993. By 1995 every state had a DUR program, and many had added online, real-time screening at the point of sale even though OBRA 90 did not require it.

The stated goal (456.703(a)) is appropriate drug therapy 'while permitting sufficient professional prerogatives to allow for individualized drug therapy'. An alert is a prompt for the pharmacist's judgment, not an automatic stop.

The seven screens, as the regulation defines them

Screen (456.705(b))Definition in plain words
(1) Therapeutic duplicationTwo or more drugs from the same therapeutic class, so the combined daily dose puts the patient at risk, or adds cost with no added benefit
(2) Drug-disease contraindicationA disease the patient has changes how the drug works, or the drug harms that disease
(3) Drug-drug interactionUsing two or more drugs together may cause, or has caused, a clinically significant adverse effect. The statute adds 'including serious interactions with nonprescription or over-the-counter drugs'.
(4) Incorrect dosageThe daily dose is outside the range in the state's standards. Dosage is the strength multiplied by the quantity dispensed divided by the days supply.
(5) Incorrect durationThe days of prescribed therapy exceed or fall short of the standards
(6) Drug-allergy interactionA significant chance of, or an actual, allergic reaction
(7) Clinical abuse or misuseAbuse, gross overuse (repeated overuse with no benefit), overutilization, underutilization, and incorrect dose or duration

The screens run against 'predetermined standards': criteria drawn from the compendia and peer-reviewed literature, agreed by physicians and pharmacists, tested against claims data so they do not throw too many false alarms, and made available to the public (456.703(d) to (g)). The pharmacy's software and the plan's claim system apply them; the pharmacist interprets the result.

Overutilization is a quantity, strength or duration greater than needed, or one that puts the patient at risk. Underutilization is too little to reach the goal. Underuse counts: a patient who fills a 30-day maintenance drug every 60 days can trigger a misuse or adherence alert, which the pharmacist follows up.

Worked example: the dose formula and the days supply

The metformin tablet label allows up to 2,550 mg a day in divided doses. A prescription for metformin 500 mg, one tablet three times a day, #90 is a 30-day supply: 500 mg × 90 ÷ 30 = 1,500 mg a day, within range.

  • Days supply entered as 15 by mistake: 500 × 90 ÷ 15 = 3,000 mg a day. The software flags a dose the patient is not taking, and the claim may be rejected.
  • Metformin 1,000 mg, #90, one tablet three times a day for 30 days: 1,000 × 90 ÷ 30 = 3,000 mg a day. This alert is real: it goes to the pharmacist, who decides whether to call the prescriber.
  • Days supply entered too long (60 instead of 30): 500 × 90 ÷ 60 = 750 mg a day. The software now underestimates the dose (a dose that is truly too high could look normal), and the next on-time refill looks early. Entered too short, it lets a refill go through early without an alert.
Exam tip Exams ask the formula directly: daily dose = strength × quantity ÷ days supply. Rearranged, days supply = quantity ÷ (units per dose × doses per day).

Counseling rules each state must set

OBRA 90 does not set national counseling rules. It requires each state to set them in state law (or another way the state Medicaid agency accepts), and it lists what those rules must include and what they must decide.

The state must decide (456.705(c)(1))Federal minimum (456.705(c)(2))
New prescriptions only, or refills tooThe pharmacist offers to counsel each patient or caregiver who presents a prescription
Whether the pharmacist must make the offer or other staff mayIn person when practicable, or by a telephone line that is toll-free for long-distance calls
Whether only refusals, or all offers, are documentedMail order pharmacies must provide toll-free service
Whether counseling itself is documentedNo counseling is required if the patient or caregiver refuses
What happens when the patient is not there (delivery, mail)The rules must say what record of a refusal the pharmacy keeps

The eight counseling matters are a menu, not a script. The pharmacist covers those he or she judges significant, consistent with state law, plus anything else significant: the name and description of the drug; dosage form, dose, route and duration; special directions and precautions for preparation, administration and use; common severe side effects, interactions and contraindications, how to avoid them and what to do if they happen; self-monitoring; storage; refill information; and what to do after a missed dose.

Safety Prep books often say 'a technician may make the offer to counsel'. That is true only where the state allows it. The counseling itself is always the pharmacist's.

Why pharmacies screen and offer for everyone

  • State law. OBRA 90 makes states write counseling standards, and many wrote them for all patients, not only Medicaid. Texas, for example, requires pharmacists to counsel on all new prescriptions, offer counseling on refills, and keep a private counseling area (Yande 2020). In a 2008 study of shoppers presenting new prescriptions in 41 states and DC, strict state rules that pharmacists counsel every patient with a new prescription predicted whether counseling happened (Kimberlin 2011).
  • Medicare. Part D plans must run concurrent DUR before each prescription is dispensed, typically at the point of sale, screening for duplication, age or gender contraindications, overuse and underuse, interactions, wrong dose or duration, drug-allergy contraindications and abuse or misuse (42 CFR 423.153(c)(2)).
  • Claims. Plans adjudicate claims online in real time, so the DUR result can come back from the insurer as well as from the pharmacy's own software (456.722 describes the Medicaid version).
  • One workflow. Running the same profile, screens and offer for every patient is simpler and safer than sorting patients by payer.

Practice falls short of the rules in studies. In observations at 45 Houston chain pharmacies (Yande 2020), most patients' first contact was a technician, the counseling window was used in under 2% of 165 encounters, and patients rarely went on to speak with the pharmacist. In the Kimberlin study, the strongest predictor of counseling was the pharmacist being the person who handed over the medicine. This is why the technician's offer, and the handoff to the pharmacist, matter.

The screen is only as good as the profile. A missing allergy or an unlisted OTC drug means the software has nothing to check against. Asking every patient, every time, is the technician's main contribution to prospective DUR.

Retrospective DUR, the DUR Board and the exemptions

  • Retrospective DUR (456.709): at least quarterly, the state analyzes claims for patterns of fraud, abuse, gross overuse or inappropriate care among physicians, pharmacists and patients, and for appropriate use of generics under state product selection laws.
  • Education (456.711): reminders to prescribers and pharmacists, face-to-face discussions with those targeted, and closer monitoring of selected prescribers or dispensers.
  • DUR Board (456.716): set up by each state; at least one-third but not more than 51% physicians, and at least one-third pharmacists; these physicians and pharmacists must be actively practicing and licensed. The Medicaid agency may accept or reject its recommendations.
  • Nursing facility residents are exempt when the facility follows the federal drug regimen review: a licensed pharmacist reviews each resident's regimen at least monthly and reports irregularities to the attending physician, medical director and director of nursing (42 CFR 483.45(c)).
  • Also exempt: drugs dispensed by a hospital using a formulary and billed at no more than its purchase cost, and drugs dispensed by HMOs (456.703(c)).

A 2020 rule added minimum Medicaid standards for opioids (456.703(h)): prospective edits on days supply for first fills, quantity, duplicate opioid fills, early refills and maximum daily morphine milligram equivalents; claims review for opioids with benzodiazepines or antipsychotics; monitoring of antipsychotic use in children; and identifying patients who might need an opioid reversal agent. Patients in hospice, palliative or cancer care and long-term care residents are excluded. These edits are a common reason for an opioid claim rejection that a technician hands to the pharmacist.

The other federal laws, in more detail

  • 1906 Pure Food and Drugs Act (FDA calls it the Food and Drugs Act): passed June 30, 1906 and signed by Theodore Roosevelt; banned interstate commerce in misbranded and adulterated foods, drinks and drugs. In 1911 the Supreme Court held it did not reach false therapeutic claims, which led to the 1912 Sherley Amendment.
  • 1938 Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act: followed Elixir Sulfanilamide, made with the poison diethylene glycol, which killed 107 people. New drugs had to be shown safe before marketing; the act also covered cosmetics and devices and authorized factory inspections.
  • 1951 Durham-Humphrey Amendment (October 26, 1951): defined drugs that cannot be used safely without medical supervision and limited them to prescription. FD&C 503(b) allows a written prescription, an oral prescription reduced promptly to writing, and refills the prescriber authorizes. Today the label of a prescription drug must bear at least 'Rx only'.
  • 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments: passed after thalidomide caused birth defects in thousands of babies in Europe (an FDA medical officer, Dr. Frances Kelsey, kept it off the US market). Manufacturers must prove effectiveness, not only safety, before marketing. FDA then reviewed drugs approved on safety alone between 1938 and 1962.
  • 1970 Controlled Substances Act: Title II of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act (October 27, 1970). Drugs are scheduled by abuse potential and accepted medical use, and everyone who handles them (manufacturers, distributors, prescribers, pharmacies) registers with DEA in a 'closed system'. DEA was created in 1973.
  • 1970 Poison Prevention Packaging Act (December 30, 1970): oral prescription drugs go in child-resistant packaging, with listed exceptions such as sublingual nitroglycerin. A non-child-resistant container may be used when the prescriber orders it or the patient asks, and child-resistant packaging is not reused. The Consumer Product Safety Commission enforces it (16 CFR 1700).
  • 1972 Drug Listing Act (August 16, 1972): firms list their marketed drugs with FDA, and each listed product has a National Drug Code: 10 digits in three segments, labeler, product and package.
  • 1983 Orphan Drug Act: a rare disease affects fewer than 200,000 people in the US (or more, if sales could not recover the cost of developing the drug). Designation brings tax credits for trials, user fee exemption and possible seven years of market exclusivity.
  • 1984 Drug Price Competition and Patent Term Restoration Act (Hatch-Waxman): bioequivalence became the basis for approving generics through the abbreviated new drug application (ANDA), without repeating the brand's clinical trials; brand companies can gain up to five years of added patent life.
  • 1990 and 2004 anabolic steroid laws: the 1990 act made anabolic steroids a controlled class (now schedule III, for example oxandrolone); the Anabolic Steroid Control Act of 2004 banned OTC steroid precursors and raised penalties.
  • 2003 Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act (MMA): created the Medicare Part D drug benefit. Part D plans must run concurrent and retrospective DUR and, for targeted enrollees, a medication therapy management (MTM) program that includes an annual comprehensive medication review by a pharmacist or other qualified provider.
  • 2005 Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act (signed March 2006): OTC ephedrine, pseudoephedrine and phenylpropanolamine products go behind the counter or in a locked cabinet. Sellers check photo ID, keep a logbook (name, address, signature, product, quantity, date, time) for at least 2 years, train staff and self-certify with DEA. Limits: 3.6 g base per day per purchaser; 9 g in 30 days, of which no more than 7.5 g by mail or carrier. A single package with 60 mg or less of pseudoephedrine needs no logbook entry.
  • 2013 Drug Supply Chain Security Act: Title II of the Drug Quality and Security Act (Pub. L. 113-54, November 27, 2013). It sets up an electronic, interoperable way to identify and trace certain prescription drugs at the package level. A trading partner, including a dispenser (pharmacy), that determines a product is illegitimate notifies FDA and its immediate trading partners within 24 hours.
Exam tip Federal law sets the floor. States often add stricter rules: counseling for every patient, which staff may make the offer, lower pseudoephedrine limits or a prescription requirement, and their own record rules. When federal and state law differ, follow the stricter one.
