CMEA: Pseudoephedrine and Other Listed Chemicals

What the CMEA covers

The Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act of 2005 (CMEA) sets federal rules for over-the-counter products that contain the List I chemicals ephedrine, pseudoephedrine or phenylpropanolamine, including their salts and isomers. These are 'scheduled listed chemical products' (SLCPs): they can be used to make methamphetamine illegally. Every pharmacy or store that sells them is a 'regulated seller'.

ChemicalWhere you see itStatus
PseudoephedrineDecongestants (30 mg, 60 mg, 120 mg 12-hour) and '-D' allergy combinationsOTC, behind the counter
EphedrineOTC asthma tablets (ephedrine sulfate 25 mg, ephedrine HCl 12.5 mg)OTC, behind the counter
Phenylpropanolamine (PPA)None for people: FDA asked makers to stop selling it in 2000 (hemorrhagic stroke)Veterinary prescription only
Phenylephrine ('PE' products)Decongestants such as Sudafed PENot a listed chemical: CMEA does not apply

The limits (always in grams of base)

7.5 g of those 9 gThe purchaserThe most that may arrive by mail or carrier
7.5 g per 30 daysMail-order sellers and mobile (kiosk) vendorsTheir own 30-day sales limit
Exam tip Federal law is the floor. Many states set lower limits, add tracking or require a prescription; follow whichever rule is stricter.

Converting tablets to base

Labels give the salt (pseudoephedrine HCl). The limits count the base. Base = salt × 0.819 (about 0.82) for the HCl salt; × 0.771 (about 0.77) for a sulfate salt.

Round down. The counts match DEA's equivalency chart.
ProductBase per tabletMost tablets in 3.6 gMost tablets in 9 g
Pseudoephedrine HCl 30 mg30 × 0.819 ≈ 24.6 mg3,600 ÷ 24.6 = 146.3 → 146366
Pseudoephedrine HCl 120 mg120 × 0.819 ≈ 98.3 mg3,600 ÷ 98.3 = 36.6 → 3691
  • One 20-count box of 120 mg: 2,400 mg salt × 0.819 ≈ 1.97 g base. A second box the same day makes 3.93 g: over the limit, so one box only.
  • One 24-count box of 30 mg: 720 mg salt × 0.819 ≈ 0.59 g base. Six boxes (3.54 g) fit in a day; a seventh does not.
Safety Do not divide 3.6 g by the salt strength. 3,600 ÷ 120 = 30 tablets is the classic wrong answer; the base math allows 36.

Making the sale

  1. Take the product from behind the counter or a locked cabinet; customers never pick it up themselves.
  2. Check a current photo ID issued by a state or the federal government (or another document on DEA's list). No ID, no sale.
  3. Logbook: the purchaser enters name, address, date and time and signs; you enter the product name and quantity.
  4. Confirm that the logbook name matches the ID and that the date and time are right.
  5. Check the day's total for this purchaser, then hand the product directly to the person who signed.

Exception: a single package with no more than 60 mg of pseudoephedrine (one 60 mg or two 30 mg tablets) needs no logbook entry or ID.

When a sale would go over the limit

  • Do not sell the part over the limit. Selling up to the limit is lawful; follow store policy.
  • Do not ring it as a second sale or under another person's name. The 3.6 g counts every sale to that purchaser that day.
  • Refuse when the ID is missing, does not match the logbook name, or the state system blocks the sale.
  • Tell the pharmacist about a refused sale or a purchase pattern that looks like diversion.
Safety Choosing another decongestant for the patient is counseling: the pharmacist does it. The technician applies the limits and records the sale.

Exam pearls

Exam tip 3.6 g a day, 9 g in 30 days, 7.5 g by mail; always as base. Logbook kept 2 years. Blister packs of no more than 2 units. Behind the counter or locked cabinet (mobile vendors: locked cabinet only). Self-certify every year, one certificate per store. Listed chemical losses go on DEA Form 107, not Form 106. Phenylephrine is not covered.

In depth

Who and what the law covers

The CMEA is Title VII of the USA PATRIOT Improvement and Reauthorization Act of 2005, signed March 9, 2006. The sales limits and blister-pack rule took effect April 8, 2006; behind-the-counter placement, the logbook, ID checks, training and self-certification took effect September 30, 2006. DEA wrote the retail rules into 21 CFR part 1314.

TermMeaning
Scheduled listed chemical product (SLCP)A product containing ephedrine, pseudoephedrine or PPA (or their salts and isomers) that may be sold lawfully as a nonprescription drug
Regulated sellerA retail distributor, including a pharmacy or a mobile retail vendor; its employees and agents are not separate regulated sellers
At retailA sale or purchase for personal use
Mobile retail vendorSells from a temporary or movable stand, such as a mall or airport kiosk
Mail-order saleAny retail sale delivered by the Postal Service or a carrier, ordered by phone, mail, fax, internet or any way other than face to face
  • Combination products count. A fexofenadine 60 mg/pseudoephedrine HCl 120 mg tablet counts 98.3 mg of pseudoephedrine base toward the limit, the same as a plain 120 mg tablet.
  • Liquids count too. Children's pseudoephedrine 15 mg/5 mL in a 118 mL bottle holds 354 mg salt, about 0.29 g base. The blister-pack rule applies only to nonliquid forms.
  • True prescription products are not SLCPs. Ephedrine sulfate injection (used for low blood pressure during anesthesia) is Rx only and outside the CMEA retail rules.
  • Where a state makes the OTC product prescription-only, DEA's Pharmacist's Manual says the pharmacy still keeps the logbook, the 3.6 g limit and its self-certification.
  • Regulated sellers do not register with DEA to sell SLCPs at retail; they self-certify. A pharmacy that distributes bulk quantities needs a separate chemical distributor registration (DEA Form 510).
Exam tip State law: the CMEA does not override a stricter state rule. Some states schedule these products as controlled substances, limit sales to pharmacy staff, or set lower daily or monthly limits. Where state and federal rules differ, the pharmacy follows the stricter of each.

The limits in detail

  • A 'day' is a calendar day, midnight to midnight, not a rolling 24 hours (DEA, 71 FR 56008).
  • A '30-day period' is rolling: it looks back over the purchaser's previous 30 days.
  • Each chemical has its own limit: 3.6 g of pseudoephedrine base, or 3.6 g of ephedrine base, in a day.
  • The daily 3.6 g binds every regulated seller and every mail-order seller. The 9 g, 30-day limit binds the purchaser; DEA states it is imposed on the purchaser, not the seller. Only mail-order sellers and mobile retail vendors have their own 30-day seller limit (7.5 g).
  • DEA's 2006 rule does not require a store-front seller to track one purchaser's sales on a single day, within the store or across its other stores. The 3.6 g limit still applies however many transactions there are, and a seller who knows at the time of sale (without consulting the logbook) that a sale goes over violates it. Many states and pharmacy systems do track; a known overage, daily or 30-day, should not be sold.
Who breaks the ruleFederal consequence
Seller who sells over 3.6 g in a dayCivil penalty when the seller knew at the time of sale that it went over; criminal penalties if the violation is knowing (21 U.S.C. 842)
Purchaser who buys more than 9 g in 30 daysUp to 1 year in prison, a fine of at least $1,000, or both (higher after a prior drug conviction)
Anyone who falsifies the logbookUp to $250,000 (individual) or $500,000 (organization), up to 5 years, or both
Seller who certifies training that did not happenFines and imprisonment under the same false-statement law (18 U.S.C. 1001)

Base conversion in depth

A salt is the drug molecule plus an acid that makes it stable and soluble; the limits ignore the acid's weight. The factor is the base's molecular weight divided by the salt's: pseudoephedrine 165.23 ÷ pseudoephedrine HCl 201.69 = 0.819. Pseudoephedrine sulfate carries two base molecules per sulfate: 2 × 165.23 ÷ 428.5 = 0.771. Ephedrine has the same molecular weight as pseudoephedrine (they are isomers), so the same factors apply.

  1. Find the salt and the strength on the Drug Facts label (for example, pseudoephedrine HCl 120 mg).
  2. Multiply by the factor: 120 mg × 0.819 = 98.3 mg base per tablet.
  3. Multiply by the number of tablets in the package: 20 × 98.3 mg = 1,966 mg, or 1.97 g.
  4. Add every SLCP package for that purchaser today, chemical by chemical, and compare with 3.6 g.

Worked basket: a purchaser brings one 20-count box of pseudoephedrine HCl 120 mg (1.97 g base) and two 24-count boxes of 30 mg (0.59 g each, 1.18 g). Total 3.15 g: the sale is allowed. A third 30 mg box would make 3.74 g, so it cannot be sold that day. Over 30 days, four 20-count boxes of 120 mg add up to 7.86 g; a fifth would make 9.83 g and put the purchaser over 9 g.

Identification

The purchaser must show an identification card with a photograph issued by a state or the federal government, such as a driver's license, state ID card or US passport. The law also accepts the identity documents listed for employment verification in 8 CFR 274a.2(b)(1)(v)(A) and (B); DEA's Pharmacist's Manual lists them, among them a permanent resident card, a US military card, Native American tribal documents and a Canadian driver's license, with school or medical records for some minors. DEA has no power to change that list. If a purchaser has no acceptable ID, the training tells employees to ask a supervisor; the sale cannot be made without it.

A seller who releases logbook information in good faith to law enforcement is protected from civil liability unless the release is grossly negligent or willful misconduct. Do not use logbook information for any other purpose or show it to anyone else. A record kept for a state law can serve as the federal logbook if it holds all the federal items. Logbook and self-certification certificate are on DEA's list of records a pharmacy must keep.

Training and self-certification

  • Who is trained: every employee who delivers SLCPs into the purchaser's hands or takes payment for them. In a pharmacy that includes technicians and front-store cashiers who ring these sales.
  • Content: the seller must use DEA's posted training material and may add its own.
  • Record: each trained employee signs an acknowledgment before selling, kept in the personnel file (71 FR 56008). The seller keeps every training record and a copy of each self-certification.
  • Self-certification: the seller certifies to DEA online that its staff are trained and that it understands and will follow the rules on limits, packaging, placement, ID and the logbook. It cannot sell SLCPs until this is filed.
  • One certificate per location. The first certificate runs 12 to 23 months (DEA assigns an expiration month); after that it is renewed every year, and the seller must have trained all employees before each renewal.
  • Fee: $21 for each certification, waived for a pharmacy with a current DEA registration to dispense controlled substances.
  • Hiring: a seller may ask job applicants whether they have been convicted of a crime involving these products or controlled substances.

Placement and packaging

  • Behind the counter means customers cannot reach the product before the sale. A locked cabinet in a customer area also counts; DEA says it should be sturdy enough that it cannot be carried off, like a cigarette case only staff can open.
  • Mobile retail vendors must use a locked cabinet.
  • The seller hands the product directly to the purchaser.
  • Nonliquid forms, gel caps included, come only in blister packs of no more than 2 dosage units per blister, or unit-dose packets or pouches where blisters are not technically feasible. Liquids are exempt from this rule.

Mail order (including pharmacy home delivery of OTC SLCPs)

  • Before shipping, get a copy of the purchaser's photo ID and check that the name and address match the order. If the product goes to someone else, verify both people; if either cannot be verified, do not ship.
  • Limits: 3.6 g base a day and 7.5 g base per 30 days to one purchaser.
  • Train everyone who prepares and packages the product or takes payment, and self-certify, as for store sales.
  • Report each sale to DEA monthly, by the 15th of the following month.
  • Not reportable: sales on a valid prescription, sales to long-term care facility residents or to the facility for its residents, and certain two-unit samples.

Theft and loss

Report any unusual or excessive loss or disappearance of an SLCP, such as a theft, to the local DEA Diversion office: by phone at the earliest practicable opportunity, then in writing within 15 days of learning of it, online on DEA Form 107 (Report of Theft or Loss of Listed Chemicals). Include the circumstances (theft from the store, in transit). For a loss in transit, the supplier reports. DEA Form 106 is for controlled substances, a different rule.

Phenylpropanolamine and ephedrine

On November 3, 2000, FDA asked makers to stop marketing PPA because of an association with hemorrhagic stroke; it was a common decongestant and diet-aid ingredient. In 2014 FDA withdrew approval of the remaining PPA drug applications (13 NDAs and 7 ANDAs). PPA is now sold only as a veterinary prescription drug (chewable tablets for urinary incontinence in dogs), so a technician meets it only in law questions. OTC ephedrine is sold as a bronchodilator for mild intermittent asthma (ephedrine sulfate 25 mg, ephedrine HCl 12.5 mg) and follows exactly the same CMEA rules as pseudoephedrine.
