Exam tip HIPAA (1996, expanded by the HITECH Act of 2009) has three rules a technician meets: the Privacy Rule (all PHI, any form), the Security Rule (electronic PHI only) and the Breach Notification Rule. The HHS Office for Civil Rights enforces them.

Try one

HIPAA in the Pharmacy practice question

At a community pharmacy, a technician has counted, labeled and bagged a refill of Zoloft 100 mg tablets. Before checking it, the pharmacist stepped into the consultation room with another patient. The patient arrives and says she is double-parked. The store has no technician-check program. What should the technician do?

  1. Release it because the dose and directions match last month's fill
  2. Have the second technician on duty check it, then release it
  3. Release it after the stock bottle scan matches the label
  4. Keep it behind the counter until the pharmacist completes the final check

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A refill that looks unchanged can still hold a counting, labeling or product error, and the profile may have changed since last month. Similarity to an earlier fill does not replace this fill's verification.
  • B. A second set of technician eyes can catch errors, but without an approved technician-check program it does not replace the pharmacist's final verification.
  • C. A barcode match confirms the product and strength the technician pulled. It is a filling safeguard, not the pharmacist's review of the prescription, the profile and the finished product.
  • D. Correct. The technician tells the patient the pharmacist will check it shortly and keeps the bag behind the counter until the pharmacist has verified it.

The NHA test plan lists making sure the pharmacist has completed final verification before a prescription is released to the patient. Federal OBRA 90 rules for Medicaid add that the pharmacist screens each prescription for drug problems and offers counseling before it is dispensed, and pharmacies apply that review to every patient. The technician's part is to keep the patient informed, give an honest wait time and let the pharmacist know a patient is waiting. Scans and peer checks help catch errors, but they are steps within filling, not the pharmacist's check.

Sources: NHA ExCPT Test Plan (2023 job analysis); 42 CFR 456 Subpart K (OBRA 90 drug use review)

Family, pickup, phone and counter

  • Patient present: share with family or a friend if the patient agrees, does not object when asked, or clearly does not mind.
  • Patient absent: the pharmacist may share what is directly relevant to that person's role, if it is in the patient's best interest.
  • Pickup: HIPAA lets the pharmacy use professional judgment to let someone else pick up filled prescriptions. ID and signature rules come from pharmacy policy and state law.
  • Voicemail: leave the least, such as the patient's name, the pharmacy and a callback number. A patient may ask to be called at another number or by another method.
  • Counter: lower your voice, keep screens and bags turned from the line, and offer a private area. Incidental overhearing despite these steps is not a violation.
Safety Send to the pharmacist or privacy official: police, subpoenas, lawyers, media, an unknown caller asking for details, a parent asking about a minor, or a patient asking to restrict sharing. Never confirm that someone is a patient to a person with no role in their care.

Disposal, security and breaches

  • Paper with PHI (labels, vial and bag stickers, receipts, faxes, hard copies) is shredded or destroyed so it cannot be read or put back together. Marking out is not destruction.
  • Electronic media are wiped or destroyed before reuse or disposal.
  • Security Rule: administrative, physical and technical safeguards. Use only your own login, lock or log off the screen, never share passwords.
  • Breach: notify each patient without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery. For 500 or more, HHS is told at the same time (media too if over 500 in one state); fewer go in a yearly report to HHS.
Safety Report a suspected breach to the privacy official at once (a bag given to the wrong patient, a misdirected fax, a lost device). The 60-day clock starts on the first day any workforce member (other than the person who caused the breach) knew, or with reasonable diligence would have known.

Card numbers and Social Security numbers

  • SSNs, card numbers, driver's license numbers and addresses are personally identifiable information (PII). Collect them only when needed and never write them on scrap paper.
  • Medicare cards now carry a Medicare Beneficiary Identifier, not the SSN; CMS says to protect it like an SSN.
  • Card verification codes (the 3 or 4 digits on the card) may not be stored after the payment is approved, even if the customer agrees (PCI DSS, an industry rule).

Who HIPAA covers, and what is not PHI

A health care provider is a covered entity only if it sends health information electronically in a standard transaction, such as a claim or an eligibility check. Federal regulations define health care to include dispensing a drug or device under a prescription, so a pharmacy that bills insurance electronically, which is nearly every pharmacy, is covered. A cash-only pharmacy that never sends a standard transaction would fall outside HIPAA, though state privacy law and board rules still apply.

SituationHow HIPAA treats it
A store or company whose business includes a pharmacy and non-health retailMay be a hybrid entity: HIPAA applies to the parts it designates as health care components, such as the pharmacy
Pharmacy software, e-prescribing gateways, billing services, shredding companiesBusiness associates: written contract required, and they are directly liable; their subcontractors are too
Technicians, interns, students on rotation, volunteersWorkforce: trained by the pharmacy and covered by its sanctions policy
The pharmacy's own employee files (as an employer)Not PHI, even if they hold health information
A patient who died more than 50 years agoNo longer PHI
De-identified data (identifiers removed by the rule's method)Not PHI; HIPAA no longer applies
Exam tip Exams like to ask whether PHI must be written down. It does not: a spoken conversation at the counter or on the phone is PHI just like a label or a screen.

Treatment, payment and operations in practice

Most of a pharmacy's daily work uses PHI for treatment, payment or health care operations, and none of it needs the patient's consent or authorization. A pharmacy may ask for consent if it wants to, but it is optional. The original 2000 rule would have required written consent before a pharmacy could fill a prescription that arrived ahead of the patient; HHS removed that requirement in 2002, before it took effect, partly for this reason.

PurposeTechnician tasks that fit
TreatmentEntering and filling a prescription, calling the prescriber's office to clarify, transferring a prescription, giving the profile to a hospital or another pharmacy caring for the patient
PaymentSubmitting and rebilling claims, eligibility checks, prior authorization paperwork, collection of a balance
Health care operationsQuality and error review, audits, training new technicians, accreditation, compliance work, resolving complaints

Minimum necessary still applies to payment and operations. For claims, HHS said a pharmacy may rely on the payer's or PBM's request as the minimum necessary when that is reasonable; disputes over how much a PBM asks for are settled between the pharmacist and the PBM. Inside the pharmacy, minimum necessary means role-based access: the pharmacy decides which staff need which PHI and limits access to it. The whole record is used or sent only when the purpose truly needs it.

  • Minimum necessary does not apply to: disclosures to a provider for treatment; disclosures to the patient; uses or disclosures under the patient's authorization; disclosures to HHS for an investigation; uses or disclosures required by law.
  • Looking up a neighbor, coworker, family member or celebrity in the pharmacy system with no work reason is a use not permitted by the rule, even if nothing is shared.

Family, caregivers and personal representatives

Who is askingWhat HIPAA allows
Family member or friend, patient present and able to decideShare what is relevant to that person's involvement if the patient agrees, is given a chance to object and does not, or the circumstances show the patient does not object
Family member or friend, patient absent or unable to decideProfessional judgment of the patient's best interest; only what is directly relevant to that person's role in care or payment
Someone picking up a filled prescriptionAllowed on professional judgment and common practice; the person learns what is in the bag, not the patient's history
Personal representative (health care power of attorney, guardian, parent of a minor in most cases)Treated as the patient for the PHI their authority covers, including access to records
Family after the patient's deathThose involved in care or payment may get what is relevant to that involvement, unless the patient said otherwise; the executor acts as personal representative
  • Minors: whether a parent can see a minor's PHI depends on state law. Where a minor may legally consent to a service on their own, the parent may not be the personal representative for that service. Refer these questions to the pharmacist.
  • A pharmacy may decline to treat someone as a personal representative if it reasonably believes the patient is or may be abused or endangered by that person.
  • Identity checks: HIPAA requires verifying the identity and authority of an unknown requester, but not for the family and caregiver sharing above. Public officials can be verified by a badge in person or government letterhead in writing.
Safety A caller who says they are the patient's spouse and asks what the patient takes may or may not be involved in the patient's care, and a full medication list is more than a pickup needs. Do not decide it yourself: take a message or transfer the call to the pharmacist.

Disclosures for the public interest

HIPAA lists purposes for which PHI may be shared without authorization or a chance to object. Each one permits a disclosure; it does not require one. Where another law, such as a state's reporting law, requires the report, that law creates the duty. In a pharmacy, these requests are handled by the pharmacist or privacy official, with a written record, because most of them must appear in an accounting of disclosures.

PurposePharmacy example
Required by lawA report a state law requires, such as dispensing data a state's prescription monitoring program collects
Public healthReports to a public health authority; adverse events, product problems and recall follow-up for FDA-regulated products
Abuse, neglect, domestic violenceReports to the agency authorized to receive them, under set conditions
Health oversightA board of pharmacy inspection, a Medicaid audit
Court and legal proceedingsA court order (only what it names); a subpoena only with assurances that the patient was notified or a protective order was sought
Law enforcementA warrant or court order; limited facts to identify or locate a suspect or missing person; more in narrow cases
Serious threat to health or safetyA good-faith belief that sharing is needed to prevent a serious and imminent threat
OthersCoroners and funeral directors, organ donation, approved research, military and national security, workers' compensation
Exam tip Exam trap: an officer at the counter asking for a patient's prescription history is not automatically entitled to it. The pharmacist or privacy official decides under the law enforcement conditions; the technician does not hand it over.

Authorizations, marketing and refill reminders

An authorization is the patient's signed permission for a use or disclosure HIPAA does not otherwise allow. A valid one describes the information, who may disclose it and who may receive it, the purpose, an expiration date or event, and the patient's signature and date. It must tell the patient they can revoke it in writing. It is invalid if it has expired, is incomplete or is known to be revoked.

CommunicationAuthorization needed?
Refill reminder or message about a drug the patient now takes, no outside paymentNo
Same reminder, a manufacturer pays only the cost of printing and mailing itNo
Same reminder, the manufacturer pays more than the cost (an incentive)Yes: it is marketing, and the authorization must say payment is involved
Reminder given face to face at the counterNo, even if paid for (face-to-face marketing is exempt)
A promotional gift of nominal value from the pharmacyNo
Selling patient lists or PHI to anyoneYes, and it must say the pharmacy is paid

The Notice of Privacy Practices

  • It must carry, as its header or otherwise prominently displayed, the statement that it describes how medical information may be used and disclosed and how the patient can get access to it.
  • It explains, with examples, uses for treatment, payment and operations, the other purposes allowed without authorization, the patient's rights, the pharmacy's duties and how to complain to the pharmacy or to HHS.
  • A provider gives it by the date of first service (as soon as practical after an emergency), keeps copies on site, posts it where patients can read it and posts it on its website if it has one.
  • The pharmacy makes a good-faith effort to get a written acknowledgment and documents why when it cannot. Not getting one does not stop the pharmacy from filling the prescription.
  • HHS said a pharmacy may collect the acknowledgment in the signature log patients already sign at pickup, if the log says clearly what is being acknowledged and the same signature is not also used as a waiver of counseling.

Patient rights in more detail

  • Access covers the designated record set (for a pharmacy, mainly prescription and billing records). The pharmacy may require written requests if it tells patients so. If the record is electronic and the patient asks for an electronic copy, it must be given in the form asked for if readily producible.
  • A patient may direct a copy to another person in a signed written request. If the patient asks for their own copy by unencrypted email after being told of the risk, the pharmacy may send it and is not responsible for unauthorized access in transit.
  • Fees for copies may cover only labor for copying, supplies, postage and an agreed summary; no other cost may be included.
  • If an amendment is denied, the patient may file a statement of disagreement, which travels with later disclosures of that information.
  • The accounting of disclosures does not list disclosures for treatment, payment or operations, to the patient, incidental ones, those under authorization, to family or caregivers under the involvement rule, for national security, to corrections or police custody, or in a limited data set.
  • Out-of-pocket restriction: if a patient pays in full and asks that the health plan not be billed or told (a disclosure for payment or operations), the pharmacy must agree unless a law requires the disclosure. HHS noted that a prescriber can give a paper prescription so the patient can ask the pharmacy before a claim is sent.
  • No one may be retaliated against or made to waive HIPAA rights as a condition of service.
Exam tip The numbers exams pair: access 30 days, amendment 60 days, accounting 60 days, each with one 30-day extension; accounting looks back 6 years. Breach notice to patients: no later than 60 calendar days after discovery.

The Security Rule

The Security Rule protects electronic PHI only. The pharmacy must keep it confidential, intact and available, guard against reasonably expected threats, and make its workforce comply. It is flexible: the size and resources of the pharmacy shape how it meets each standard. Specifications marked required must be done; those marked addressable must be done if reasonable, or an equal alternative documented.

Safeguard typeExamples from the ruleWhat the technician does
AdministrativeRisk analysis, sanctions policy, security training, incident procedures, backup and disaster plansComplete training, follow policy, report incidents
PhysicalFacility access, workstation use and security, disposal and reuse of devices and mediaKeep screens from public view, secure devices, never take PHI home without approval
TechnicalUnique user ID (required), emergency access, automatic logoff, encryption, audit controlsUse your own login only; log off or lock; remember that access is audited

HHS proposed a major update to the Security Rule in January 2025. As of November 2026 the Federal Register shows it as a proposed rule, not final, so the current rule above still applies.

Breach notification

A breach is a use or disclosure of PHI that the Privacy Rule does not allow and that compromises it. Any such event is presumed a breach unless a documented risk assessment shows a low probability of compromise, weighing what PHI was involved, who received it, whether it was actually viewed or taken, and how well the risk was reduced. Notice is owed only for unsecured PHI; PHI properly encrypted, or destroyed, does not trigger it.

Not a breachExample
Unintentional, good-faith access by a workforce member within their job, with no further misuseA technician opens the wrong profile, closes it and tells no one
Inadvertent sharing between two people at the same entity who are both allowed to see PHIA pharmacist sends a note to the wrong coworker in the same pharmacy
The recipient could not reasonably have kept the informationA bag handed to the wrong person and taken back at once, unopened
Who is notifiedWhen

HHS, fewer than 500A log, reported within 60 days after the end of the calendar year
Prominent media in the stateMore than 500 residents of one state, same 60-day limit

Safety Destroyed means shredded or destroyed so the PHI cannot be read or reconstructed. HHS's guidance says redaction, such as a marker over a label, is not destruction. Labeled vials, bags and receipts go in the pharmacy's secured PHI disposal bin (shredding or a destruction service), never the open trash.

Enforcement, penalties and paperwork

ConsequenceDetail
Civil money penalties (HHS)Four tiers by culpability: did not know; reasonable cause; willful neglect corrected within 30 days; willful neglect not corrected. Amounts are adjusted for inflation every year
Criminal (knowingly obtaining or disclosing)Up to $50,000 and 1 year; under false pretenses up to $100,000 and 5 years; to sell or for gain or malicious harm up to $250,000 and 10 years. Employees can be charged personally
Employer sanctionsEvery covered entity must have and apply sanctions against workforce members who break its privacy policies, and document them
  • Each covered entity names a privacy official and a contact for complaints, trains all workforce members (new staff within a reasonable time), and mitigates harm from a violation it knows of.
  • HIPAA documents (policies, notices, acknowledgments, complaints, sanctions) are kept 6 years from creation or last effective date. That is not the retention period for prescription records, which comes from state law and, for controlled substances, DEA rules.
  • A workforce member who in good faith reports unlawful or unsafe conduct to an oversight agency, an accreditation body or their own lawyer does not violate HIPAA, and the pharmacy may not sanction them for it.

State law and other federal privacy laws

  • HIPAA is a floor. A state privacy law that is more stringent (it restricts a disclosure HIPAA allows, or gives patients greater access or more information) stays in force. So do state laws for reporting disease, injury or child abuse and for public health surveillance; a contrary state controlled-substance law survives only through an HHS exception determination.
  • Records from a federally assisted substance use disorder program have extra federal protection under 42 CFR Part 2.
  • Proposed changes are not law: the 2021 Privacy Rule proposal (care coordination, shorter access times) and the January 2025 Security Rule proposal were both still proposals in the Federal Register as of November 2026.

Other personal information (PII)

NIST defines PII as information that can identify a person (name, SSN, date and place of birth, biometrics) or is linked to one (medical, financial, employment). In a pharmacy, much PII is also PHI because it sits in the patient's record; payment card data is guarded by the card networks' rules as well.

  • Ask for an SSN only when a process truly needs it; most insurance and Medicare transactions use plan member IDs and the MBI instead.
  • Read card numbers and SSNs back quietly or not at all; never say them aloud at the counter or leave them on a sticky note or the will-call bag.
  • Phone payments: enter the card directly into the system; card verification codes may not be kept after approval, even encrypted or with permission.
  • Copies of insurance cards and IDs are stored and destroyed like any other PHI.
  • Report a lost or exposed card number, ID or SSN to the privacy official the same as a PHI breach.

Exam pearls

  • PHI is any form: oral, paper or electronic. The Security Rule covers only electronic PHI.
  • Treatment, payment and operations need no authorization; marketing (most often paid for by a third party) and sale of PHI do.
  • Minimum necessary does not apply to treatment disclosures to providers or disclosures to the patient.
  • A friend may pick up a filled prescription on the pharmacy's professional judgment; HIPAA does not require written permission from the patient.
  • Incidental disclosures (overheard despite reasonable safeguards) are not violations; careless ones are.
  • Shred, do not just mark out. Report suspected breaches immediately.
  • Patients can see and copy their records, ask for corrections, get an accounting of certain disclosures, request restrictions and confidential communications, and complain without retaliation.
