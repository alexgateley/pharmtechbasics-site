Try one

Prescription Intake and Processing practice question

In a hospital IV room, a technician receives an order for cefazolin 1 g IVPB q8h for a surgical patient. Which preparation matches the route written in the order?

  1. The dose in a syringe for slow injection over 3 to 5 minutes
  2. The dose added to a 1,000 mL bag to run continuously
  3. The dose diluted in a 50 mL bag for intermittent infusion
  4. The dose in a syringe for deep injection into a large muscle

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because the cefazolin label allows direct injection over 3 to 5 minutes, but that route is IV push (IVP), not IV piggyback.
  • B. Tempting because a large-volume bag is also given intravenously, but a continuous infusion is not intermittent dosing every 8 hours; IVPB is a small-volume bag.
  • C. Correct. IVPB means intravenous piggyback: the dose goes in a small-volume bag that is infused intermittently through the patient's primary IV line.
  • D. Tempting because cefazolin can be given intramuscularly, but that route would be written IM; IVPB names an intravenous route.

IVPB (intravenous piggyback) is an intermittent infusion: a small-volume bag, commonly 50 to 100 mL, hung as a secondary line that runs into the primary IV line over a set time. The cefazolin label describes three ways to give the drug: intramuscular injection, direct IV injection slowly over 3 to 5 minutes, and intermittent or continuous infusion after the reconstituted vial is diluted in 50 to 100 mL of a compatible fluid such as sodium chloride 0.9% or dextrose 5%. The q8h frequency means one bag every 8 hours, three doses in 24 hours, so the IV room labels each bag with its own administration time.

Sources: Cefazolin for injection label (Hikma, 2026)

Prescriber authority and the completeness check

  • A prescriber may prescribe only what the state license allows (and, for controlled drugs, with a DEA registration or exemption): a dentist for dental care, a podiatrist for the foot, a veterinarian for animals, a dermatologist for the skin.
  • Out-of-scope orders (a dentist's blood pressure drug, a veterinarian writing for a person) go to the pharmacist. The technician does not decide.
  • Check: right patient (second identifier such as date of birth), every element present, legible, quantity and directions make sense, signed, dated.
  • Missing or unclear: tell the pharmacist; with permission, call the prescriber's office for it. Never guess, and never add a signature.
  • Forgery flags (DEA): too neat, different inks or handwriting, photocopied, odd quantities or directions, directions written out in full.

Patient profile

  • Demographics: name, address, phone, date of birth, gender
  • Allergies and drug reactions (record the reaction, not just the drug)
  • Conditions (disease states)
  • Every medication and device, including OTC drugs, vitamins and herbals
  • Insurance: every plan, primary first, with BIN, PCN, group, cardholder ID, person code
Exam tip OBRA '90 (42 CFR 456.705) sets the minimum Medicaid profile: demographics, disease states, allergies and reactions, a full medication and device list, and pharmacist comments.

Exam tip Coordination of benefits: bill the primary plan, then send what is left to the secondary. Medicaid pays last. Tiers: lower tier, lower copay (generics low, preferred brand middle, non-preferred higher, specialty highest).

Days supply

Days supply = quantity dispensed ÷ quantity used per day. For as-needed directions, use the most the directions allow.

  1. Metformin 500 mg, 2 tablets twice daily, #120: 2 × 2 = 4 a day; 120 ÷ 4 = 30 days.
  2. Lantus 10 mL vial (100 units/mL = 1,000 units), 20 units daily: 1,000 ÷ 20 = 50, but an opened vial lasts 28 days, so 28 days.
Safety A wrong days supply causes false refill-too-soon rejects, wrong DUR dose checks and a claim that misstates what was dispensed.

Controlled-substance prescriptions in more detail

  • Validity: a controlled prescription must be issued for a legitimate medical purpose by a practitioner in the usual course of practice. The prescriber is responsible, and the pharmacist who fills it has a corresponding responsibility (21 CFR 1306.04).
  • Who may issue: a practitioner authorized by the state where licensed and registered with DEA, or exempt (for example military and Public Health Service practitioners, or a resident using a hospital's registration with an internal code number) (21 CFR 1306.03, 1306.05(g) and (h)).
  • A secretary or nurse may prepare the prescription for the prescriber's signature, but the prescriber stays responsible (21 CFR 1306.05(f)).
  • Paper: ink, indelible pencil, typed or computer-printed, and manually signed; a computer-generated prescription that is printed or faxed must still be manually signed (21 CFR 1306.05(d)).
  • GHB (sodium oxybate) prescriptions must state the patient's medical need on the face (21 CFR 1306.05(c)).
  • The old DATA-waiver 'X' identification number is no longer required: DEA removed 21 CFR 1306.05(b) in a rule published June 9, 2026 (effective July 9, 2026), after Congress ended the waiver program in the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023.
  • Schedule II: no refills. A prescriber may issue several Schedule II prescriptions totaling up to a 90-day supply, each one after the first marked with the earliest date it may be filled, if state law allows (21 CFR 1306.12).
  • Schedules III and IV: refills only if authorized, at most five, within six months of the date issued (21 CFR 1306.22).
  • There is no federal time limit for filling a Schedule II prescription; state law may set one, and the pharmacist must judge that the patient still needs it.
Exam tip DEA numbers: practitioner numbers start with A, B or F, mid-level practitioners with M; the second letter is usually the first letter of the prescriber's last name, followed by seven digits (DEA Pharmacist's Manual).

Safety If a technician answers a call that turns out to be a new prescription and the state does not let technicians take one, the technician gets the pharmacist rather than writing it down.

Spotting a prescription that is not right

Quantities, directions or doses unlike usual practicePatient returns too often for a monthly supply
Textbook-style or no standard abbreviations; directions written out in fullDepressants and stimulants written at the same time
PhotocopiedPrescriptions in other people's names
Different inks or different handwritingSeveral people with similar prescriptions from one prescriber, or from out of the area

Prevention (DEA): know the prescribers, their signatures, DEA numbers and agents; know the patients; check that the prescription was presented a reasonable time after it was written; ask for identification when in doubt. Large quantities alone are not proof of fraud: a patient on long-term opioid therapy may need them.

Safety The technician's job is to notice and tell the pharmacist. The pharmacist decides whether to call the prescriber, refuse the prescription or contact police.

The NDC in full

  • 21 CFR 207.33: every listed drug package has a unique NDC of three segments. Labeler code (assigned by FDA), product code (the drug, strength and dosage form for that labeler), package code (package size and type).
  • 10-digit layouts in use today: 4-4-2, 5-3-2 or 5-4-1. The current rule text also permits 6-digit labeler codes (6-4-1 or 6-3-2, 11 digits), but FDA says it will keep assigning 10-digit NDCs until the 12-digit rule takes effect. Each labeler uses only one product-package layout.
  • Billing: HIPAA transactions use an 11-digit 5-4-2 NDC with no hyphens. Add a leading zero to whichever segment is short: 0088-2220-33 becomes 00088222033.
  • Each manufacturer, repackager and relabeler puts its own labeler code on its packages, so the same tablet can carry several NDCs. Bill the NDC on the bottle you actually used.
  • An NDC is not FDA approval, does not prove the product is a drug, and does not mean a plan covers it (FDA NDC Directory page).
  • Coming change: FDA's March 2026 final rule makes every NDC 12 digits (6-4-2) on March 7, 2033, by adding leading zeros to old codes. Labels may keep 10-digit NDCs until March 6, 2036. FDA says the rule does not itself change the 11-digit billing format.
Exam tip Product selection is checked by NDC, lot number and expiration date. A scanned NDC that does not match the one entered is a stop: the wrong drug, strength or package size has been picked.

  • Refill too soon: plans allow a refill once most of the last supply should be used. CMS's GLP-1 Bridge tool, for example, allows it once more than 75% of the days supply would be used. Some plans are stricter for opioids: Medi-Cal Rx lists 90% for opioid claims and returns early refills as a DUR reject (88) rather than 79. Check the plan's message.
  • Prior authorization: the plan's approval before it pays. Step therapy is a kind of prior authorization: try a cheaper drug first. Quantity limits cap how much is covered in a period, for example 30 tablets a month.
  • Exceptions: the patient or prescriber can ask the plan to cover a non-formulary drug, waive a rule or charge a lower tier; the prescriber must send a supporting statement.
  • Medicare Part D transition fill: a new enrollee may get a one-time 30-day supply of a drug they already take that the plan does not cover or restricts.
  • Before a patient leaves without the medication, involve the pharmacist: options such as a cash price or a short supply are the pharmacist's call.

DUR alerts: what they are and whose they are

Prospective drug utilization review (DUR) screens each prescription before it is filled. For Medicaid, federal rules (42 CFR 456.705) list the checks: therapeutic duplication, drug-disease contraindication, drug-drug interaction, incorrect dose, incorrect duration, drug-allergy interaction, and clinical abuse or misuse. Pharmacy software and claims processors run the same kinds of checks.

  • The dose check uses strength × quantity ÷ days supply, so a mistyped days supply can raise a false high-dose or low-dose alert.
  • A drug-allergy alert depends on the allergy being on the profile: an empty allergy field is not the same as 'no known allergies'.
  • The technician can fix data errors (wrong drug picked, wrong days supply, a missing allergy). The clinical decision on any real alert belongs to the pharmacist; a technician never overrides it.
Safety Never clear or override a DUR alert to make a claim go through. Hand it to the pharmacist.

Coordination of benefits and copays

  • Each coverage is a payer. The primary payer pays up to its limits, then the balance goes to the secondary payer; what neither pays is the patient's (Medicare.gov).
  • Medicaid is the payer of last resort: bill every other plan first (Virginia Medicaid payer sheet).
  • If a claim rejects 41, the plan knows of other coverage: ask the patient for every card.
  • Tiers: each plan sets its own. A typical Medicare example: Tier 1 most generics (lowest copay), Tier 2 preferred brands, Tier 3 non-preferred brands, specialty tier for very high-cost drugs (highest).
  • Copay is a set amount; coinsurance is a percentage of the drug cost, so it rises when the price rises.
  • When a plan adds a generic or biosimilar, it may move the brand to a higher tier, and a patient who stays on the brand pays more.
Exam tip The order to know: verify every coverage, bill the primary, bill the secondary with the primary's response, then collect what the patient owes.

Days supply: more worked examples

PrescriptionWorkingDays supply
Ventolin HFA 200 inhalations, 2 inhalations every 4 to 6 hours as neededMost allowed: every 4 hours = 6 doses × 2 = 12 a day; 200 ÷ 12 = 16.716
Lantus SoloStar, 5 pens × 3 mL (1,500 units), 30 units dailyThe pen's instructions add a 2-unit safety test before every injection: 30 + 2 = 32 units a day; 1,500 ÷ 32 = 46.9. Each pen (300 units) lasts about 9 days, inside its 28-day in-use limit46
Lantus 10 mL vial, 50 units daily1,000 ÷ 50 = 20; used up before 28 days20
Amoxicillin 500 mg, 1 capsule three times daily for 10 days, #3030 ÷ 3 = 1010
  • As needed (PRN): base it on the most the directions allow.
  • Round down to a whole day; rounding up makes the next refill come due before the supply runs out.
  • Check the label for a discard time after opening or mixing (insulin vials and pens, for example): the supply cannot last longer than the product does once opened. For insulin pens, count the priming (safety test) dose the pen's instructions call for.
  • Plans cap the days supply per fill, and many insurers limit controlled substances to a 30-day supply (DEA manual); a fill over the cap rejects 76.
Safety Enter the days supply the directions give, not the one the plan will pay. Changing it to get a claim through misstates the claim.

Exam pearls

  • DEA number on controlled prescriptions; NPI on claims.
  • Schedule II: no refills, emergency oral only from the prescriber personally, written follow-up within 7 days.
  • Schedule III-IV: up to 5 refills within 6 months.
  • DAW 1 = prescriber says brand; DAW 2 = patient wants brand; DAW 0 = no selection, usually generic.
  • 11-digit NDC: add a leading zero to the short segment (5-4-2).
  • BIN routes the claim, PCN picks the plan at that processor, person code picks the family member.
  • Reject 79 refill too soon, 75 prior authorization, 76 plan limits, 70 not covered, 88 DUR, 41 bill another payer.
  • Primary first, then secondary; Medicaid last.
  • Days supply = quantity ÷ amount used per day; PRN uses the maximum.
  • Out-of-scope prescriptions, DUR alerts and therapeutic decisions go to the pharmacist.
