Turning a sig into patient directions

Build the label in this order: verb, amount and form, route, how often, how long, and why. Write whole words, no Latin and no abbreviations. Under federal law the label carries the directions for use from the prescription; the pharmacist checks every translation.

SigLabelQuantity
1 cap po tid x 7dTake 1 capsule by mouth three times a day for 7 days.1 × 3 × 7 = 21 capsules, 7-day supply
2 gtt OU qidPlace 2 drops in each eye four times a day.2 × 2 eyes × 4 = 16 drops a day
1 tsp po q6h prn coughTake 5 mL by mouth every 6 hours as needed for cough.Up to 4 doses: 20 mL a day at most
Safety Convert teaspoons to mL on the label and send a dosing device marked in mL. If a code is unclear (a 'u' that could be a zero, OD on an oral liquid), do not guess: the pharmacist clarifies with the prescriber.

Roman numerals

IVXLCDM
1510501005001,000

A repeated letter repeats its value (III = 3). A smaller letter after a larger one adds (VI = 6, LX = 60); before it, subtracts (IV = 4, IX = 9, XL = 40, XC = 90). Examples: XIV = 14, XXX = 30, XC = 90, CXX = 120.

Exam tip Old-style quantities may be written 'Disp #XC' (90) or 'tabs ii' (2). Doses in Roman numerals are error-prone: enter Arabic numerals. Controlled substance schedules are CII to CV.

Who translates the sig, and what the label must carry

'Sig' is short for signatura, the part of the prescription that says how the patient uses the drug. The ExCPT test plan lists translating the prescriber's directions into accurate, complete patient directions as a technician task, often done through the software's sig codes. NCPDP describes that translation as historically the pharmacist's professional prerogative, which is why the pharmacist verifies every one.

  • Federal law (21 U.S.C. 353(b)(2)) exempts a dispensed prescription drug from most of the act's drug labeling requirements when its label bears the dispenser's name and address, the prescription's serial number, the date of the prescription or of its filling, the prescriber's name, the patient's name if stated on the prescription, and the directions for use and any cautionary statements contained in the prescription.
  • Most states require more on the label than this federal minimum. Follow your state's rules; they can be stricter than federal law.
  • Sig codes in pharmacy software expand to full words. NCPDP asks that any code or default for an oral liquid print the dose in mL.
  • NCPDP's Universal Medication Schedule goes further than whole words: 'take 1 pill in the morning and 1 in the evening' instead of 'twice daily'. Use it where your pharmacy has adopted it.
Safety A technician does not change the meaning of a sig. Missing pieces (no frequency, no strength, a dose that looks wrong) and unclear codes go to the pharmacist, who clarifies with the prescriber.

Two more worked examples. Sig 'ii tabs po qhs, disp #LX': Take 2 tablets by mouth at bedtime; LX is 60, so 60 ÷ 2 a day = a 30-day supply. Sig 'insulin glargine U-100, 20 units SUBQ qhs': Inject 20 units under the skin at bedtime. Had the prescriber written '20u', the u could hide a zero (200), so the pharmacist would clarify it.

  • Every other day: qod for 30 days is 15 doses, not 30.
  • Once-weekly sigs: count weeks, not days (1 tablet once weekly for 4 weeks is 4 tablets).
  • Two-phase sigs (twice daily for 7 days, then once daily) are counted phase by phase, then added.
  • Eye drops: drops a day divided into drops in the bottle gives days supply. 20 drops per mL is an estimate; real droppers and liquids differ, so follow the pharmacy's and the plan's rules.
  • For a prn sig, days supply is usually figured from the most doses the sig allows (q6h prn: up to 4 a day). Plan rules vary.

Frequency codes: the fine print

  • bid and q12h are not the same. NCI's definitions: bid is two times a day at unspecified times; q12h is every 12 hours by the clock.
  • q4h allows up to 6 doses in 24 hours, q6h 4, q8h 3, q12h 2.
  • Coined frequency forms are error-prone too. ISMP's example: q6PM, meant as every evening at 6 PM, has been read as every 6 hours; write 'daily at 6 PM'.
  • A capital Q with lowercase hs (Qhs) has been read as qhr, every hour; ISMP's advice is QHS or qhs, and the label says 'at bedtime'.
  • ac and pc tie a dose to meals: 'before meals' or 'after meals' on the label, with 'three times a day' if the sig says tid ac.

  • NCPDP: oral liquid labels use mL only, with a leading zero (0.5 mL), no trailing zero (5 mL, 2.5 mL), a space between number and unit, and a matching device marked in mL; an oral syringe is generally preferred up to 10 mL.
  • Teaspoon and tablespoon mix-ups cause threefold errors; household spoons are not measuring devices. NCPDP's example: a label reading 3.5 teaspoons for an ordered 3.5 mL gave a fivefold overdose.
  • USP deleted its teaspoon chapter in 2010, and current USP and ONC standards no longer treat household units as official, as NCPDP describes.
  • Units (insulin, heparin) and mEq (milliequivalents, for example potassium) are not metric prefixes; never abbreviate 'units'.
  • Write commas at 1,000 and above in US labels; some countries use a comma as the decimal point, so never use a decimal comma.

Error-prone abbreviations: the two lists

ISMP's List of Error-Prone Abbreviations, Symbols, and Dose Designations (2024) collects items reported to its error reporting program that were misread in harmful or potentially harmful errors. ISMP says they should never be used, spoken, typed or handwritten, including on prescriptions, computer-generated labels, bin labels and order-entry screens. The list is copyrighted: read it from ISMP.

  • The Joint Commission's Do Not Use list sits under its information management standard IM.02.02.01. Accredited organizations (hospitals and others) must put its items on their own do-not-use list; ISMP marks those items with a double asterisk.
  • In 2006 FDA and ISMP ran a national campaign to stop error-prone abbreviations, and FDA's 2022 labeling guidance points manufacturers to the ISMP list.
  • Drug names: ISMP says not to abbreviate them (MTX, HCTZ, APAP are examples). NCPDP's work helped move prescription labels from 'APAP' to 'acetaminophen'.
  • D/C can mean discharge or discontinue. An order like 'D/C clopidogrel' for a patient about to go home is ambiguous: the pharmacist clarifies it.
  • Symbols are on the list as well: a handwritten @ or & can look like a number, so write 'at' and 'and'.
  • ISMP's scope is not limited to hospitals: it names prescriptions and computer-generated labels, so the same habits apply in community, mail-order and long-term care pharmacy.
Safety When you type a label, never add an abbreviation the patient could misread, even if the prescriber used one. Write the full words and leave the clinical question (is the dose right?) to the pharmacist.

Roman numerals in more depth

  • Six subtractive pairs are in common use: IV (4), IX (9), XL (40), XC (90), CD (400), CM (900). Older tables show other forms (GPO lists IC for 99), but these six are what pharmacy quantities use.
  • Read left to right, adding, and subtract where a smaller numeral stands before a larger one: XCIV = 90 + 4 = 94; CXLV = 100 + 40 + 5 = 145.
  • A bar over a numeral multiplies it by 1,000 (GPO Style Manual). ISMP notes that M has been used for both thousand and million: write the word.
  • Handwritten prescriptions sometimes use lowercase numerals for counts (i, ii, iii) and ss for one-half. Enter Arabic numerals and words ('2 tablets', 'half tablet').
  • FDA recommends CIV rather than C-IV on labels of injectable schedule IV drugs, because '-IV' can be read as intravenous or as 4 mg.

Exam pearls

  • OD is the right eye (or, error-prone, 'once daily'); AU is each ear; OU is each eye.
  • qid is four times a day; qd (error-prone) is daily; qod (error-prone) is every other day.
  • tid x 10 days of 1 tablet = 30 tablets; bid x 14 days = 28.
  • 1 tsp = 5 mL; 1 tbsp = 15 mL; 1 kg = 2.2 lb; 1 mg = 1,000 mcg.
  • Always lead, never follow: 0.5 mg, never .5 mg; 5 mg, never 5.0 mg.
  • Write 'units', 'daily', 'every other day' and 'morphine sulfate' in full.
  • XC = 90, XL = 40, LX = 60. Doses in Roman numerals are entered as Arabic numerals.
  • Patient directions use whole words and start with a verb: Take, Place, Apply, Insert, Inhale, Inject.
